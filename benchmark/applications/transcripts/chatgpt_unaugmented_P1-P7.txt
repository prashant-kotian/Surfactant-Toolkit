## Bottom line

The only publication matching the numerical formulation reports 6% “SDS”, 16% n-butanol, 1:1 aqueous phase:oil and the following Winsor-III windows:

- n-octane: 3.0–6.6 g NaCl/100 mL
- n-decane: 4.0–8.5 g/100 mL
- n-dodecane: 6.0–11.8 g/100 mL [mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9717/14/17/2693?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Using those data with the ionic-HLD relation \(\ln S^*=a+k\,\mathrm{EACN}\), my conditional predictions are:

| Oil | EACN used | Predicted balanced \(S^*\), g NaCl/100 mL | Input-consistent uncertainty range | 25 °C assessment |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| n-Hexane | 6 | **3.18** | **2.16–4.66** | Attainable |
| n-Heptane | 7 | **3.73** | **2.56–5.42** | Attainable |
| n-Tetradecane | 14 | **11.39** | **8.10–16.58** | Attainable |
| n-Hexadecane | 16 | **15.66** | **10.93–23.29** | Attainable, but watch for surfactant crystallization |
| Ethyl palmitate | 6.8 | **3.62** | **2.04–6.26** | Salinity attainable, but **not a reliably liquid oil at exactly 25 °C** |

These are engineering predictions, not published measurements.

## Critical provenance problem

The matching paper repeatedly identifies its surfactant as **sodium dodecyl sulfonate**, not sodium dodecyl sulfate. [mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9717/14/17/2693?utm_source=chatgpt.com) These are distinct compounds:

- Sodium dodecyl sulfate: CAS 151-21-3, \( \mathrm{C_{12}H_{25}NaO_4S}\), 288.38 g/mol. [151-21-3](https://www.tcichemicals.com/IN/en/p/D1403?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
- Sodium 1-dodecanesulfonate: CAS 2386-53-0, \( \mathrm{C_{12}H_{25}NaO_3S}\), 272.38 g/mol. [2386-53-0](https://www.tcichemicals.com/IN/en/p/D1222?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Therefore:

> The table above applies to sodium dodecyl **sulfate** only if “sulfonate” in Bai et al. is a naming error and their actual reagent was CAS 151-21-3. Until the authors’ reagent catalogue number or CoA is verified, the literature does not establish an exact sulfate calibration.

The publication also reports percentages without establishing, in the accessible methods text, that both are specifically w/v. That is another exact-composition uncertainty.

## Derivation

For ionic surfactants at fixed temperature and alcohol content, HLD gives

\[
\ln S^*=a+k\,\mathrm{EACN},
\]

with \(S\) in equivalent grams NaCl/100 mL. Reported anionic-surfactant values are approximately \(k=0.15\)–0.17. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666934X21000325?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The Bai paper supplies Winsor-III intervals, not experimentally reported balanced points. Since the model is linear in \(\ln S\), I used the geometric centre of each interval—the minimax estimate when the unknown balanced point is known only to lie inside that interval:

\[
\sqrt{3.0(6.6)}=4.4497,\quad
\sqrt{4.0(8.5)}=5.8310,\quad
\sqrt{6.0(11.8)}=8.4143.
\]

Regression against EACN \(=8,10,12\) gives

\[
\boxed{\ln S^*=0.20260+0.15927\,\mathrm{EACN}}
\]

or

\[
\boxed{S^*=\exp(0.20260+0.15927\,\mathrm{EACN})}.
\]

The fitted slope, 0.1593, lies inside the independently reported 0.15–0.17 range.

For ethyl palmitate I used the experimentally tabulated **EACN = 6.8** from Ontiveros et al. [researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236836110_Classification_of_ester_oils_according_to_their_Equivalent_Alkane_Carbon_Number_EACN_and_asymmetry_of_fish_diagrams_of_C10E4ester_oilwater_systems?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### Uncertainty construction

The alkane ranges are worst-case envelopes, not nominal 95% confidence intervals. I varied \(k\) over 0.15–0.17 and allowed the intercept to take every value for which the predicted octane, decane and dodecane optima remain inside all three published Winsor-III windows:

\[
\ln L_i\le a+kN_i\le\ln U_i.
\]

For ethyl palmitate the primary EACN paper reports no uncertainty. I therefore widened the calculation using ±1.16 EACN units, the leave-one-out RMSE reported for an independent EACN estimation model. [mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-8015/2/4/19?utm_source=chatgpt.com) The resulting 2.04–6.26 range is a conservative sensitivity envelope, not a statistical confidence interval.

## Physical attainability at 25 °C

Saturated aqueous NaCl at 25 °C is approximately 26.48 wt% with density 1.1978 g/mL, corresponding to

\[
100(1.1978)(0.2648)=31.7\ \text{g NaCl per 100 mL solution}.
\] :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"}


Thus every predicted point and even every upper uncertainty bound is below the **pure-water NaCl saturation ceiling**. This is only a necessary condition: 16% n-butanol and 6% SDS can lower salt or surfactant solubility, so stock clarity must be checked experimentally.

The four n-alkanes are liquids at 25 °C; even n-hexadecane melts near 291.3 K, about 18.1 °C. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="8"}

Ethyl palmitate is different. NIST reports fusion temperatures from 297.15 to 298.2 K, with assigned uncertainties of 2–4 K. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="9"} At exactly 25 °C it can be crystalline, partly molten or supercooled depending on purity and thermal history. Consequently, its predicted salinity is chemically reachable, but a reproducible equilibrium experiment with a single liquid “oil phase” is **not guaranteed at 25 °C**. A proper liquid-oil measurement should be performed safely above its melting range, for example 30 °C, and treated as a different-temperature prediction.

### Preparation

For each salinity, prepare the aqueous formulation to final volume at 25.00 °C:

- 6.000 g active SDS
- 16.00 g n-butanol
- \(S\) grams NaCl
- water to exactly 100.00 mL

Use \(m_\text{weighed}=m_\text{active}/\text{assay fraction}\). Reject or record separately any formulation showing NaCl crystals, SDS crystals or a separate butanol-rich layer before oil is added.

Place **5.00 mL aqueous formulation + 5.00 mL oil** in a sealed, graduated tube with minimal headspace. For hexane, sealing is essential because evaporation changes both the 1:1 ratio and apparent optimum.

Mix at 30 rpm for 2 h, matching the published procedure, then hold at \(25.00\pm0.10\ ^\circ\mathrm C\). :chatgpt-content-reference{index="10"} Read phase volumes after 24 and 48 h and continue until every interface changes by less than 0.05 mL over 24 h.

For ethyl palmitate, pre-melt it, charge the tubes, then equilibrate at 25 °C. Record crystals photographically. Crystallization means the 25 °C liquid-oil prediction is physically inapplicable.

### Decisive endpoint

Classify each tube as Winsor I, III or II. For each clean Winsor-III tube measure the upper excess-oil volume \(V_o^e\) and lower excess-brine volume \(V_w^e\). With equal initial oil and aqueous volumes, the operational balance condition is

\[
D(S)=V_o^e-V_w^e=0.
\]

Locate two adjacent salinities where \(D\) changes sign and interpolate linearly in \(\ln S\). For a definitive component balance—especially if n-butanol partitioning visibly changes excess-phase volumes—measure water in the middle phase by Karl Fischer and oil by GC-FID; balance is where the middle phase contains equal solubilized oil and water volumes.

A prediction is falsified by that single scan if:

1. no Winsor-III tube occurs anywhere across the stated grid;
2. the measured \(D=0\) salinity lies outside the stated uncertainty range; or
3. the required aqueous formulation cannot be prepared without salt or surfactant precipitation.

Before interpreting a failure as an HLD-model failure, resolve the sulfate-versus-sulfonate identity of the published calibration.


Problem 2 — Sub-CMC aggregate census for C12E8 and SDS: 
## Result

A unique monomer/dimer/trimer census cannot be extracted from a CMC measurement. CMC data constrain the overall onset of aggregation, but not the individual oligomer equilibrium constants. The tables below are therefore explicitly **model predictions**, not measured populations.

My preferred estimates are:

- For C12E8: the published 25 °C polydisperse molecular-thermodynamic model, recalibrated only along its concentration axis to the experimental CMC.
- For SDS: a cooperative mass-action ladder constrained by the experimental CMC and micellar aggregation number. The nonionic C12E8 free-energy model cannot be transferred unchanged to SDS.

“Mass fraction” means fraction of the total surfactant formula units in each state. Because every unit has the same molecular mass, this is also the mole fraction of surfactant molecules assigned to that state.

### C12E8 calculation

I evaluated the published distribution

\[
X_N=N X_1^N
 \exp\!\left[-\frac{N\Delta\mu_N^0}{RT}\right],
\]

with

\[
a_N=\left(\frac{36\pi v^2}{N}\right)^{1/3},\qquad
\Delta\mu_N^0=-2\gamma a_0+
 \frac{\gamma}{a_N}(a_N-a_0)^2,
\]

for \(N=2,\ldots,56\), and solved

\[
C_T=C_1+\sum_{N=2}^{56} C_N
\]

numerically. These equations and their physical interpretation are given in the original study. [izbi.uni-leipzig.de](https://www.izbi.uni-leipzig.de/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/La98.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Direct evaluation gives a 50%-aggregated concentration of 0.140 mM rather than the measured 0.084 mM. I therefore applied a uniform derived transfer-free-energy correction of \(-1.26\ {\rm kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), equivalent to shifting the concentration scale to the measured CMC. This does not change the predicted normalized size distribution at a given \(C_T/\mathrm{CMC}\).

## SDS census

For SDS I used the transparent cooperative ladder

\[
c_n=\sigma K^{n-1}c_1^n,\qquad n\ge2,
\]

so, defining \(x=Kc_1\),

\[
KC_T=x+\sigma\frac{x^2(2-x)}{(1-x)^2}.
\]

The aggregate-number mean is

\[
\bar n_{\rm agg}=\frac{2-x}{1-x}.
\]

I required \(c_1=\mathrm{CMC}\) and equal monomer/aggregated mass at \(C_T=2\,\mathrm{CMC}\), while \(\bar n_{\rm agg}=62\). These conditions derive

\[
x_*=\frac{N-2}{N-1}=0.983607,\qquad
K=\frac{x_*}{\mathrm{CMC}}=121.4\ {\rm M^{-1}},
\]

\[
\sigma=\frac{(1-x_*)^2}{x_*(2-x_*)}
       =2.688\times10^{-4}.
\]

## Structural model uncertainty

Two other defensible limiting models give much wider answers:

1. **Closed \(N\)-mer association:** monomer plus one micelle size. It is maximally cooperative and predicts exactly zero dimer and trimer.
2. **Isodesmic association:** every monomer-addition step has the same equilibrium constant, with \(K=1/\mathrm{CMC}\). It maximizes small oligomers but cannot reproduce the measured, relatively well-defined micelle aggregation numbers.

## Consistency checks and physical assessment

- Every row satisfies
  \[
  f_1+f_2+f_3+f_{n\ge4}=1
  \]
  to numerical precision better than \(10^{-9}\).

- The C12E8 calculation independently gives the expected monomer plateau: between 1 and 2 CMC, total concentration doubles while free monomer changes only from 0.0420 to 0.0432 mM.

- The C12E8 spherical model predicts mean aggregate sizes below the measured \(70\pm4\); the original authors themselves found approximately 50 from their spherical distribution and noted disagreement with the directly measured value and likely nonspherical micelles. [izbi.uni-leipzig.de](https://www.izbi.uni-leipzig.de/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/La98.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Thus I trust its total monomer-versus-aggregate partition more than its exact dimer/trimer ratio.

- For SDS, the preferred model predicts about 99% monomer at 7 mM, consistent with the NMR-diffusion result that 7 mM SDS was monomeric. The experiment found increasing hydrodynamic aggregation between roughly 7 and 25–30 mM. [researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350947104_Study_of_the_premicellar_state_in_aqueous_solutions_of_sodium_dodecyl_sulfate_by_NMR_diffusion?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

- The SDS NMR signal was a fast-exchange average, not separate peaks for monomers, dimers and trimers. Therefore its “average aggregation number” cannot uniquely validate any particular census.

- An alternative empirical analysis has shown that SDS conductivity, NMR, diffusion and surface-tension data can be fitted without explicitly postulating premicellar species. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021979711015268?utm_source=chatgpt.com) That is why the closed-association result remains a legitimate lower bound.

## What transfers to ionic SDS—and what does not

Applicable to both:

- conservation of surfactant formula units;
- chemical-equilibrium/mass-action bookkeeping;
- concentration-dependent coexistence of monomers and aggregates;
- a distribution rather than a mathematically sharp “no micelles/micelles” boundary.

Not directly transferable to SDS:

- C12E8’s neutral-headgroup free energy;
- ideal concentration in place of ionic activity;
- a concentration-independent stepwise association constant;
- omission of sulfate–sulfate repulsion, Na\(^+\) binding, ionic-strength feedback and counterion condensation.

For SDS, \(K\) and \(\sigma\) above are therefore effective parameters for pure water near the CMC, not molecular constants. Added salt, buffer, probe ions or significant pH-dependent impurities would invalidate the census.

The most defensible conclusion is consequently not that one table is “the measured truth,” but that the preferred calculations put both systems overwhelmingly in the monomer state at the lowest concentrations, while the exact amount and size distribution of sub-CMC aggregation remains strongly model-dependent. The C12E8 dimer and trimer values are probably reliable only to order of magnitude; the SDS values are even less secure because the ionic activity and counterion problem is not independently constrained.

## Model

For a new planar interface,

\[
\Gamma(t)=2c_\infty\sqrt{\frac{Dt}{\pi}}
-\sqrt{\frac{D}{\pi}}\int_0^t
\frac{c_s(\tau)}{\sqrt{t-\tau}}\,d\tau ,
\]

coupled to

\[
bA\,c_s=\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}e^{-2a\theta},
\qquad
\theta=\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma_m},
\]

and

\[
\gamma(t)=\gamma_0+nRT\Gamma_m
\left[\ln(1-\theta)+a\theta^2\right].
\]

These are the Ward–Tordai mass balance and Frumkin equation of state used in the SDS study by Kinoshita, Parra and Needham. Their paper also explains why \(n=2\) applies to an uncorrected salt-free ionic surfactant, while the activity-renormalized representation is treated as an electroneutral \(n=1\) interface. [researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317153002_Adsorption_of_ionic_surfactants_at_microscopic_air-water_interfaces_using_the_Micropipette_interfacial_area-expansion_method_Measurement_of_the_diffusion_coefficient_and_renormalization_of_the_mean_io?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

At \(10^{-2}\) M the total concentration exceeds the CMC. I therefore used \(c_\infty=8.2\) mM for diffusible monomer; excess SDS is micellar. A recent 25 °C measurement found 8.23 mM by streaming potential and 8.18 mM by conductivity, with literature values 7.9–8.2 mM. For droplets of diameter \(d\), I calculated the isolated rise velocity by force balance with the finite-Reynolds-number Schiller–Naumann drag law,

\[
C_D=\frac{24}{Re}\left(1+0.15Re^{0.687}\right),\qquad

\[
u(d)=u_\infty(d)(1-\phi)^{4.48},\qquad \phi=0.05.
\]

The authors measured the DSD by laser diffraction, reported predominantly monomodal, positively skewed, approximately lognormal distributions, and found Tween-80 spans of 0.592–1.335. ## 3. Coalescence constraint

The paper’s stability experiment stored the collected dispersed phase in the dark and remeasured it after 24 h, 7 d and 30 d after redispersion. The paper does not report a static 10 cm creaming experiment; its tabulated aging examples were at approximately 425–430 rpm, not specifically 330 rpm. [researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379563347_Liquid-liquid_two_phase-system_stabilized_by_tween_40_and_80_surfactants_multiparametric_study?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

More importantly, unchanged DSD alone gives no assumption-free upper bound on coalescence: coalesced oil could leave the sampled droplet population while the surviving droplets retain the same DSD. A quantitative bound is possible only under a stated population-balance model.

Define an effective constant kernel by

\[
\frac{dN}{dt}=-\beta N^2,\qquad
\frac{d_{32}(t)}{d_{32}(0)}
  =\left(1+\beta N_0t\right)^{1/3}.
\]

For the central lognormal DSD,

\[
N_0=\frac{6\phi e^{3s^2}}{\pi d_{32}^3}
   =1.12\times10^{10}\ {\rm m^{-3}}.
\]

Taking the paper’s largest reported sizing standard deviation, 10 μm, as the maximum undetected increase gives \(\delta=10/235=0.0426\), and therefore

\[
\beta_{\max}=
\frac{(1+\delta)^3-1}{N_0(30\,{\rm d})}
=4.57\times10^{-18}\ {\rm m^3\,s^{-1}}.
\]

Across the reported span range:

\[
\boxed{\beta_{\max}=3.4\text{–}6.0\times10^{-18}\ {\rm m^3\,s^{-1}}}.
\]

If \(K\) denotes the conventional Smoluchowski kernel with \(dN/dt=-KN^2/2\), then

\[
\boxed{K_{\max}=6.8\times10^{-18}\text{–}1.20\times10^{-17}\ {\rm m^3\,s^{-1}}}.
\]

Using a more conservative \(2\times10~\mu\mathrm m\) detection threshold raises that to \(1.42\text{–}2.49\times10^{-17}\ {\rm m^3\,s^{-1}}\). These are model-conditional bounds, not measured rate constants; the kinetic convention follows the classical Smoluchowski population balance. [degruyterbrill.com](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/zpch-1918-9209/html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Even at the upper bound, diameter growth during \(t_{50}\) is only \(2\times10^{-7}\) fractionally. The predicted \(t_{50}\) changes by less than about 5 μs. Consequently, permitted coalescence has no meaningful effect on the seconds-scale creaming curve.

## Falsifying experiment

Use one fresh batch in a transparent, thermostated 10.0 cm column of reported internal diameter:

1. Begin video or multi-height optical-transmission acquisition within one second of stopping agitation; acquire at ≥10 Hz for the first two minutes.
2. Measure the local oil-volume-fraction profile \(\phi(z,t)\), rather than relying only on cream-layer height, and integrate the excess top-layer oil to obtain \(F(t)\).
3. The creaming prediction is falsified if \(t_{50}\) falls outside 8.2–17.6 s under the stated drag model, or if the measured curve falls systematically outside the tabulated band.
4. Continue storing the same closed column at 25 °C; measure the whole-sample, redispersed DSD at day 0 and day 30. Growth exceeding 10 μm falsifies the operational coalescence bound. Simultaneous droplet-number measurements would test the constant-kernel assumption directly.
5. Record cream, serum and free-oil layers separately. A persistent cream with unchanged DSD confirms coalescence stability but does not refute the predicted rapid gravitational creaming.

A stricter post-publication stress test is less favorable: a newer study found two transitions for nominal C12E7 at 54.4 and 88.9 °C, whereas this model predicts one crossing at 65.1 °C. The residual relative to the lower transition is +10.7 °C, outside the claimed ±5 °C performance. [ACS Publications](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/full/10.1021/acs.jced.5c00682?utm_source=chatgpt.com) The double transition and supplier-grade sample are warning signs that a one-parameter, monodisperse HLD model is missing relevant phase behavior.

## 4. Where not to trust these predictions

Do not use these numbers without an experimental scan for:

- Commercial or polydisperse ethoxylates; the nominal average EO number does not reproduce the behavior of a pure ethoxymer.
- Branched or secondary alcohol ethoxylates, alkylphenol ethoxylates, mixed surfactants, or capped EO chains.
- More than about eight EO units—the linear \(C_c=2.4+0.28n-j\) rule becomes unreliable beyond this range. [api.pageplace.de](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780128214824_A62364940/preview-9780128214824_A62364940.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
- High salinity, salts other than NaCl, or specific-ion effects where \(bS\) is no longer adequate.
- Oils other than pure \(n\)-alkanes unless their EACN has been measured.
- Concentrated lyotropic phases, solutions near or below the CMC, or systems showing two cloud points.
- Dynamic emulsion inversion where heating rate, oil/water ratio, droplet size and hysteresis control the observed inversion.

For purchasing decisions, C12E5 deserves particular caution: its predicted cloud point and its hexane PIT are only a few degrees above 25 °C, so modest impurity, salt or batch-composition changes could move the formulation across a phase boundary.

PROBLEM 6 — THERMODYNAMIC CONVENTIONS FOR MICELLIZATION
=======================================================

Bottom line
-----------
The published dodecylguanidinium chloride value is reproduced by the
counterion-binding-corrected mole-fraction convention

    ΔG°mic = (1 + β) RT ln XCMC.

For SDS, using cmc0 = 0.0083 mol/L and counterion dissociation α =
0.272 ± 0.017 (therefore β = 1 − α = 0.728 ± 0.017), literature-supported
conventions span −11.87 to −43.64 kJ/mol. The convention recommended for
cross-paper comparison is the corrected mole-fraction convention,

    ΔG°mic = (2 − α) RT ln XCMC,

which gives −37.70 ± 0.37 kJ/mol from the stated inputs.

Constants and standard states
-----------------------------
Temperature T = 298 K. The molar gas constant used was
R = 8.314462618 J mol−1 K−1, so RT = 2.47731 kJ/mol. For dilute water,
55.4 mol/L was used to convert molarity to approximate solute mole fraction:

    XCMC ≈ CMC / 55.4.

The reference state is part of the convention. A molarity expression uses
c/c° with c° = 1 mol/L. A mole-fraction expression uses dimensionless X.

1. Dodecylguanidinium chloride reproduction
--------------------------------------------
Literature inputs from Bouchal et al.:

    CMC = 6.2 mmol/kg water = 0.0062 mol/kg
    counterion binding degree β = 0.74
    reported ΔG°mic = −28.3 ± 0.9 kJ/mol

For one kilogram of water, nwater ≈ 1000/18.01528 = 55.508 mol. The dilute
surfactant mole fraction is therefore

    XCMC = 0.0062 / (55.508 + 0.0062)
         = 1.1169 × 10−4,

and

    ln XCMC = −9.0997.

Using the corrected mole-fraction expression,

    ΔG°mic = (1 + β) RT ln XCMC
           = 1.74 × 2.47731 × (−9.0997)
           = −39.22 kJ/mol.

This does NOT reproduce −28.3 kJ/mol. The numerical value −28.3 kJ/mol is
instead reproduced by the convention written by Bouchal et al. for their
ionic surfactant analysis,

    ΔG°mic = (2 − β) RT ln XCMC,

where β is their stated counterion-binding fraction. Numerically,

    ΔG°mic = (2 − 0.74) × 2.47731 × (−9.0997)
           = −28.40 kJ/mol,

which differs from their reported central value by only 0.10 kJ/mol.

Thus the convention that reproduces the publication is the Bouchal/Phillips
form (2 − β)RT ln XCMC with β interpreted as the binding degree. This is
algebraically equal to (1 + α)RT ln XCMC because α = 1 − β. The notation is
easy to confuse with the more common corrected expression (1 + β)RT ln XCMC
= (2 − α)RT ln XCMC; the two are not the same.

If the only uncertainty propagated is the quoted ±0.9 kJ/mol publication
uncertainty, the reproduction is −28.40 kJ/mol and is plainly consistent with
−28.3 ± 0.9 kJ/mol. The paper's total ±0.9 kJ/mol cannot be reconstructed from
the rounded CMC and β alone without their covariance and individual input
uncertainties.

2. SDS under substantiated conventions
---------------------------------------
Inputs:

    cmc0 = 0.0083 mol/L
    α = 0.272 ± 0.017
    β = 0.728 ± 0.017
    XCMC ≈ 0.0083/55.4 = 1.4982 × 10−4
    ln(0.0083) = −4.7915
    ln(XCMC) = −8.8060

The table gives model/convention results, not separate measurements.

Convention                         Formula                         ΔG°mic (kJ/mol)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Molarity, surfactant-only          RT ln(cmc/c°)                  −11.87
Mole fraction, surfactant-only     RT ln XCMC                     −21.82
Molarity, binding-corrected        (1+β)RT ln(cmc/c°)             −20.51 ± 0.20
Mole fraction, binding-corrected   (1+β)RT ln XCMC                −37.70 ± 0.37

Bouchal/Phillips numerical form    (2−β)RT ln XCMC                −27.75 ± 0.37
when β means binding               = (1+α)RT ln XCMC

For the rows involving α or β, the displayed uncertainty is the first-order
contribution from σalpha = σbeta = 0.017:

    σ(ΔG) = RT |ln aCMC| σα,

with aCMC represented by either c/c° or X according to the row. The CMC was
given without an uncertainty, so none was invented. These bands therefore do
not include CMC, temperature, nonideality, or water-density uncertainty.

3. Spread and predominant practice
----------------------------------
Across all substantiated rows in the table, the total convention-only spread
for SDS is

    (−11.87) − (−43.64) = 31.77 kJ/mol.

The surfactant literature surveyed most often uses a mole-fraction standard
state for micellization and, for ionic surfactants, includes a counterion term.
However, symbol definitions vary: some papers use α for counterion
dissociation, others use β for binding, and a swapped coefficient changes the
answer by about 10 kJ/mol here. A numerical value without its formula, standard
state, and definition of α or β is therefore not reliably comparable.

4. Recommendation
-----------------
Report ΔG°mic = (2 − α)RT ln XCMC for a 1:1 ionic surfactant, together with
CMC, temperature, α, the XCMC conversion, and the explicit formula; for the
given SDS inputs this is −37.70 ± 0.37 kJ/mol from α uncertainty alone.

PROBLEM 7 — BLIND PREDICTION OF THE CMC OF SODIUM HEXADECYL SULFATE
==================================================================

Chronology is preserved: the prediction was fixed first without using an STS
measurement, the method was then checked on shorter homologues, and only then
were STS measurements consulted.

1. Pre-registered prediction (no measured STS value used)
---------------------------------------------------------
Predicted CMC for sodium hexadecyl sulfate (sodium cetyl/hexadecyl sulfate,
C16 sulfate) in pure water at 25 °C:

    CMCpred = 5.2 × 10−4 mol/L

with a semi-empirical prediction interval

    3.3 × 10−4 to 8.3 × 10−4 mol/L.

The model is the Klevens homologous-series relation

    log10(CMC/M) = A − B nC,

where nC is the number of alkyl-chain carbon atoms. It was anchored to SDS
(C12) at 8.3 × 10−3 M and used B = 0.300 ± 0.050 per carbon, a prior covering
the classic approximately twofold CMC decrease per added methylene for ionic
surfactants. Thus

    CMC(C16) = 0.0083 × 10^[−0.300(16−12)]
             = 5.24 × 10−4 M.

The stated interval follows from B = 0.300 ± 0.050 while holding the SDS anchor
fixed:

    lower = 0.0083 × 10^(−0.35×4) = 3.30 × 10−4 M
    upper = 0.0083 × 10^(−0.25×4) = 8.30 × 10−4 M.

This is a semi-empirical homologous-series prediction, not a first-principles
molecular simulation and not a measurement.

2. Credibility check on compounds selected before consulting STS data
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The same C12 anchor and B = 0.300 were used without refitting each homologue.

3. Held-out STS literature comparison, performed after fixing the prediction
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Comparable published STS values found after the prediction was fixed include
approximately:

    3.6–5.2 × 10−4 M near 35.8 °C, depending on method/interpretation
    4.2 × 10−4 mol/kg near 50 °C
    about 4.5 × 10−4 M in quoted compilations
    about 5.5 × 10−4 M under some nominal aqueous conditions

Against representative values, the blind prediction 5.24 × 10−4 M is:

    about 19% above the midpoint of 3.6–5.2 × 10−4 M;
    about 25% above 4.2 × 10−4;
    about 16% above 4.5 × 10−4; and
    about 4.7% below 5.5 × 10−4.

All of these fall inside the predeclared 3.3–8.3 × 10−4 M interval. The
comparison is not perfectly isothermal because the key physical complication
is that sodium hexadecyl sulfate has a Krafft temperature around 40–45 °C,
depending on purity, hydration state, and measurement protocol.

Crucial physical qualification
-------------------------------
At 25 °C, which is below the reported Krafft region for ordinary sodium
hexadecyl sulfate, an equilibrium clear micellar solution generally cannot be
prepared at the predicted concentration. The hydrated crystalline surfactant's
solubility is lower than the hypothetical micellization threshold. Therefore
5.2 × 10−4 M should be read as the extrapolated intrinsic/hypothetical micellar
CMC of the homologous dissolved state, or as a metastable value if a
supersaturated solution can be created—not as an assurance that equilibrium
micelles exist in pure water at 25 °C. A phase-solubility/Krafft measurement is
needed alongside any nominal CMC measurement.

4. Molecular assumptions and failure modes
------------------------------------------
The model assumes:

* A constant incremental hydrophobic free-energy contribution per added
  methylene group, which produces the linear Klevens relation.
* The same sulfate headgroup, sodium counterion, temperature, aqueous standard
  state, and broadly similar counterion binding across the homologous series.
* A dilute solution, no added electrolyte, no cosolvent, and no significant
  specific impurities.
* The observed break is true micellization rather than precipitation,
  hydration/dehydration, a liquid-crystalline transition, or premicellar
  aggregation.

The prediction can fail if:

* 25 °C lies below the Krafft temperature—as it does here—so crystallization
  pre-empts equilibrium micellization.
* Trace shorter-chain homologues depress the apparent transition or dominate
  surface-sensitive methods.
* Counterion binding changes nonlinearly with chain length.
* Activity coefficients, finite solubility, polymorphism, hydration state, or
  kinetic supersaturation become important.
* The analytical method assigns a precipitation or premicellar feature as the
  CMC.

Falsification experiment
------------------------
Equilibrate rigorously purified STS in sealed pure water at 25.00 °C over a
concentration series spanning 2 × 10−4 to 1.2 × 10−3 M. Measure dissolved
concentration after filtration or ultracentrifugation, identify solids by
powder X-ray diffraction or DSC, and obtain conductivity plus a second micelle
probe such as fluorescence or static light scattering. The prediction is
falsified as an equilibrium 25 °C CMC if the dissolved concentration plateaus
below the predicted interval without a micellar transition—which is the likely
outcome because of the Krafft constraint. Above the Krafft temperature, a
consistent break outside 3.3–8.3 × 10−4 M would falsify the homologous-series
numerical prediction itself.

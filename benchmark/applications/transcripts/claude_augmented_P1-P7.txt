################################################################################
PROBLEM 1 — Winsor-III (optimal) salinity for a 6% SDS + 16% n-butanol formulation
################################################################################

THE SOURCE DATASET (AND ONE IMPORTANT DISCREPANCY)
The exact formulation — 6% SDS + 16% n-butanol, NaCl brine, 1:1 brine:oil, ambient T —
is from Bai, Liu, Wang & Jian, Processes 2026, 14(17), 2693 (doi:10.3390/pr14172693).
They report the Winsor-III (middle-phase) salinity windows for three alkanes:

  Oil          ACN   Winsor-III window (g NaCl/100 mL)   Window center = my S* estimate
  n-octane      8    3.0 – 6.6                            4.80
  n-decane     10    4.0 – 8.5                            6.25
  n-dodecane   12    6.0 – 11.8                           8.90

FLAG: the paper's "SDS" is sodium dodecyl SULFONATE, not sulfATE. The brief says
sulfate. These are different molecules with different head-group hydration and,
critically, different salt tolerance. The correlation below is calibrated to the
paper's surfactant; if the colleague truly uses the sulfate, expect a systematic
offset in absolute salinities and a LOWER precipitation ceiling.

BUILDING THE PREDICTOR (derived)
- Taking S* = window center is a derived assumption. The true balanced optimum is
  where the middle phase solubilizes equal volumes of oil and water. The paper's
  core-flood effluent peaked at 6.6 g/100 mL for decane vs a window center of 6.25,
  so the real optimum may sit ~0.3–0.4 unit ABOVE center (small upward bias, noted).
- HLD for ionic surfactants (Salager et al. 1979, SPE J 19:107; Aubry et al. 2020,
  Adv. Colloid Interface Sci. 276:102099) gives, at the optimum: ln S* = K·EACN + C.
- OLS fit on the three points: K = 0.154 per carbon, C = 0.319 (residual s = 0.037
  in ln units, 1 dof). The fitted K = 0.154 is an independent sanity check: the
  literature EACN coefficient for anionic surfactants is ~0.16. That agreement is the
  main reason the log-linear form is trusted over a plain linear-in-S* fit.

PARTS 1 & 2 — PREDICTIONS WITH UNCERTAINTY

  Oil               EACN (source)                 Predicted S* (g/100 mL)   ±1σ stat band   Linear-model (sensitivity)
  n-hexane          6 (= ACN, by definition)      3.5                       3.3 – 3.7       2.6
  n-heptane         7 (= ACN)                     4.1                       3.9 – 4.2       3.6
  ethyl palmitate   6.8 (measured)                3.9                       3.4 – 4.6       3.4
  n-tetradecane     14 (= ACN)                    11.9                      11.3 – 12.6     10.8
  n-hexadecane      16 (= ACN)                    16.3                      15.0 – 17.6     12.8

EACN of ethyl palmitate = 6.8 is sourced: "Classification of ester oils according to
their EACN…C10E4/ester oil/water systems," J. Colloid Interface Sci. 2013
(ScienceDirect S0021979713003585; same table gives ethyl myristate 5, ethyl oleate 7).
KEY POINT: ethyl palmitate is a C18 molecule but behaves like a ~C7 alkane because the
ester group is polar. Feeding its carbon count of 18 into the correlation would give
S* ≈ 22 g/100 mL — completely wrong. The real answer is ~3.9, essentially heptane.

WHAT THE ±1σ BANDS DO AND DON'T CAPTURE
The statistical bands come from the 3-point regression (for ethyl palmitate widened
for EACN transferability). They do NOT capture these systematic terms, which dominate
for the extrapolated oils:
 - Model form (ln vs linear): negligible for hexane/heptane/ester, but ±3 units for
   hexadecane. This is the real uncertainty at C16, not the ±1.5 statistical band.
 - EACN transferability for ethyl palmitate: its 6.8 was measured in a nonionic C10E4
   system; polar oils show the largest surfactant-dependence, so σ_EACN ≈ ±1 was folded
   in (hence 3.4–4.6). Alkane EACNs carry no such uncertainty (EACN ≡ ACN).
 - Sulfate vs sulfonate: an unquantified offset on all five.
 - Temperature (ambient vs exactly 25 °C): ionic optimal-salinity T-coefficient ~0.01/°C
   → <5% effect, negligible here.

PART 3 — PHYSICAL-ATTAINABILITY AUDIT AT 25 °C (two ceilings)
(a) NaCl solubility. At 25 °C NaCl saturates at 36 g/100 g water = 26.5 wt%
    (≈32 g/100 mL solution, ≈36 g/100 mL water). NONE of the five predictions exceed
    this — even hexadecane at 16.3 has headroom. NaCl solubility is NOT the binding
    constraint for any of these oils.
(b) Surfactant salt tolerance (Krafft precipitation) — the real limiter. Pure sodium
    dodecyl sulfate's Krafft temperature crosses 25 °C near ~1 M NaCl (~5.8 g/100 mL)
    and it precipitates above ~2 g/100 mL (arXiv:2311.03544; Keshavarzi et al.,
    Colloids & Interfaces 2020, 4:9). The 16% n-butanol + oil strongly depress the
    Krafft point — the paper demonstrably reached a clean middle phase at 11.8 g/100 mL
    (dodecane). So the empirically proven attainability ceiling for THIS formulation is
    ~12 g/100 mL, with unknown headroom above.

  Verdict:
   - n-hexane (3.5), n-heptane (4.1), ethyl palmitate (3.9): ATTAINABLE.
   - n-tetradecane (~12): ATTAINABLE but at the very edge of the demonstrated window.
   - n-hexadecane (~16): DOUBTFUL. The salt dissolves (16 < 26), but the optimum lies
     beyond any demonstrated salt tolerance of this surfactant system at 25 °C. With a
     true sulfate head group the likely failure mode is the surfactant salting out
     before the film can be driven to balance, so a clean Winsor III at the predicted
     salinity may NOT exist at 25 °C. Falsifiable (see Part 4).

PART 4 — FALSIFYING EACH PREDICTION WITH ONE PHASE SCAN PER OIL
Setup (identical across vials for a given oil): 6% SDS + 16% n-butanol, 1:1 brine:oil
(e.g. 5 mL + 5 mL), thermostatted at 25.0 °C, equilibrated to constant phase volumes
(days, not the paper's 2 h). Positive control of n-decane to confirm you reproduce the
4.0–8.5 window (center ~6.25); if not, the discrepancy is in setup (surfactant
identity/purity or temperature), not the predictions.
Salinity ladders (~0.5 g/100 mL steps, finer near the predicted optimum):
  hexane 2.0→5.5 · heptane / ethyl palmitate 2.5→6.0 · tetradecane 8→16 · hexadecane 10→20
Readout/criteria:
  1. Sequence: Winsor I → III → II as salinity rises; no middle phase anywhere falsifies
     the model assumption for that oil.
  2. Location: at each salinity measure excess-oil / middle / excess-water volumes;
     optimum = equal oil & water solubilization (Healy–Reed), window center as a coarse
     proxy. Measured optimum outside the predicted band ⇒ prediction FALSIFIED.
  3. Ethyl-palmitate test is the sharpest: optimum near heptane confirms the EACN
     approach; an optimum far higher falsifies transferability of the nonionic-measured
     EACN into this anionic system.
  4. Hexadecane doubles as the attainability test: surfactant precipitation / no clean
     middle phase across 10–20 g/100 mL confirms the "not attainable" call; a clean
     Winsor III near 16 falsifies it.

INPUTS: SOURCED vs DERIVED
Sourced (literature):
 - Three optimal-salinity windows for the exact formulation — Bai et al., Processes 2026,
   14:2693 (surfactant = sodium dodecyl sulfonate).
 - EACN(ethyl palmitate) = 6.8 — ester-oils EACN paper, J. Colloid Interface Sci. 2013.
 - EACN(n-alkane) ≡ carbon number — definition of the EACN scale (Aubry et al. 2020).
 - Ionic HLD log-linear form and benchmark K ≈ 0.16 — Salager et al. 1979; Aubry et al. 2020.
 - NaCl solubility 26.5 wt% at 25 °C — EngineeringToolbox; Wikipedia "Saline water."
 - SDS Krafft/salt tolerance — arXiv:2311.03544; Keshavarzi et al. 2020.
Derived (by me):
 - S* = window center (4.80, 6.25, 8.90) and the regression ln S* = 0.154·EACN + 0.319.
 - The five predictions and uncertainty bands; attainability audit; falsification protocol.

Lowest-confidence numbers: hexadecane (extrapolation + likely precipitation) and the
sulfate-vs-sulfonate offset on all five — both settled directly by the single scans.

PROBLEM 2 — Sub-CMC aggregate census for C12E8 and SDS at 25 °C
################################################################################

MODEL AND PARAMETERS
Micellization is cooperative self-assembly, so a multiple-equilibrium (size-distribution)
model is required (the textbook single-n mass-action model defines oligomers to be zero).
Israelachvili/Tanford self-assembly distribution:
  X_N = N · X1^N · exp(−N·δ_N),  δ_N = (μ°_N − μ°1)/kT  (per-molecule aggregation free energy)
with X1 fixed by conservation ΣX_N = X_total. For δ_N the standard "opposing forces" form
δ_N = δ_hc(1−N^−1/3) + δ_hg(N^1/3−1) is used: hydrophobic burial ∝ surface/volume (N^−1/3,
correct for compact aggregates) plus a head-group penalty. Two constants δ_hc, δ_hg are
fixed by two sourced anchors — CMC and aggregation number — so NO free parameters.

Sourced inputs:
 - C12E8: CMC = 8×10^-5 M at 25 °C (ChemEurope/Wikipedia citing Drummond et al. 1985;
   commercial values 4.8–11×10^-5); n_mic ≈ 120 (Sigma/Calbiochem cite 123); nonionic.
 - SDS: CMC = 8.27 mM, n_mic = 62, counterion binding β ≈ 0.65 (MP Biomedicals/VWR; classic).
 - Water 55.5 mol/L (mole-fraction ↔ molar conversion).
Derived: δ_∞ = ln X_CMC = −13.45 (C12E8); δ_hc = −21.2, δ_hg = +0.87 by calibration
(minimum of δ_N lands exactly at N = 120). All aggregate populations below are COMPUTED,
model-conditional numbers — not measurements.

PARTS 1 & 2 — C12E8 CENSUS (primary model)

  C/CMC   C_total(mM)   free monomer(mM)   monomer%   dimer%    trimer%    n≥4 %
  0.05    0.0040        0.0040             99.94      0.057     0.00014    ~0
  0.10    0.0080        0.0080             99.89      0.113     0.00056    ~0
  0.12    0.0096        0.0096             99.86      0.136     0.0008     ~0
  0.25    0.0200        0.0199             99.71      0.282     0.0035     0.0001
  0.50    0.0400        0.0398             99.42      0.561     0.014      0.0008
  1.00    0.0800        0.0644             80.5       0.736     0.029      18.7
  2.00    0.1600        0.0657             41.1       0.383     0.016      58.5

Readout: below the CMC the surfactant is 99.4–99.9% monomer; dimers rise roughly linearly
with concentration from ~0.06% to ~0.56%, trimers 50–100× rarer, n≥4 negligible. Micelles
switch on abruptly at the CMC (n≥4 jumps 0 → 19% → 59%); the dimer population peaks near the
CMC (~0.7%) then falls. Free monomer tracks total below the CMC, then saturates at
≈0.065 mM (≈0.8×CMC) and stays flat above it — the classic nonionic signature.

PART 3 — DEFENSIBILITY, CONSISTENCY CHECKS, AND SPREAD
Four independent checks:
 1. Conservation — every row sums to 100.000% (ΣX_N = X_total enforced and verified).
 2. CMC sharpness / monomer saturation — monomer ≈ total below CMC, sharp break at CMC,
    free monomer plateauing near CMC. This is the discriminating test (below).
 3. Dimerization constant — K2 = X2/X1² ≈ 7.9×10³ (mole fraction) = ~140 M^-1, a reasonable
    hydrophobic-association constant for a C12 chain. Not an outlier.
 4. Consistency with the premise — a 0.1–0.6% dimer population changes monomer activity by
    <0.6%, so it is invisible to surface-tension/interfacial methods: the model explains
    exactly the phenomenon raised (real sub-CMC aggregates IFT cannot see).

The spread:
 - Parameter uncertainty (n_mic 90–130, CMC 5–11×10^-5): dimer fractions move only ~±40%
   (dimer at 0.5×CMC spans 0.47–0.78%).
 - Functional form (the dangerous one): a pairwise-contact (1−1/N) + linear head-group model
   gives δ2 = −6.8 and predicts dimers of 10–30% below the CMC — ~50× larger.
I trust the N^−1/3 (surface/volume) model and REJECT the pairwise one, because the latter
predicts monomer declining from ~89% already at 0.05×CMC — i.e. NO sharp CMC. C12E8 has a
sharp CMC, so check #2 falsifies the high-oligomer model. Robust, model-independent
conclusions: (a) sub-CMC oligomers are nonzero but minor; (b) dimer >> trimer >> higher;
(c) the absolute dimer level is uncertain by up to 1–2 orders of magnitude — and that is
exactly what IFT cannot pin down. So "dimers are present at the sub-percent level below CMC"
is defensible; any specific sub-percent number is model-conditional.

PART 4 — SDS: WHAT TRANSFERS AND WHAT BREAKS
What transfers (hydrophobic/geometric part):
 - Same C12 tail as C12E8, so δ_hc and the N^−1/3 geometry carry over.
 - The monomer/micelle split is anchored by the measured CMC: below CMC monomer ≈ total
   (0.41, 0.83, 0.99, 2.07, 4.14 mM at 0.05–0.5×CMC); micelles appear sharply at ~8.3 mM.
What does NOT transfer (electrostatic part) — demonstrated, not asserted:
 - Pseudophase relation becomes ΔG°_mic/kT ≈ (1+β) ln X_CMC. Plugging the bare ln X_CMC into
   the nonionic model smears the CMC and over-predicts dimers (~6% at CMC); plugging the
   (1+β)-scaled value (−14.5) over-stabilizes and predicts ~95% micelles at 0.05×CMC —
   absurd. NEITHER scalar fix works → a single size-independent δ cannot represent ionic
   self-assembly.
 - Missing physics: a size- and ionic-strength-dependent electrostatic free energy. A
   dimer/trimer carries 2–3 unneutralized charges (β→0 for tiny aggregates), paying an
   electrostatic penalty (~0.5–1 kT per headgroup) the nonionic model omits. Charge
   SUPPRESSES sub-CMC oligomers. A tail-plus-charge decomposition gives δ2 ≈ −3.8 vs
   C12E8's −4.1 → SDS dimers ≈ 0.4–0.5× the C12E8 value at equal C/CMC.
 - Above-CMC monomer behaves oppositely: for C12E8 free monomer stays ~flat; for SDS it
   DECREASES above the CMC (micelles sequester counterions), so monomer at 2×CMC is below
   the CMC. The nonionic model cannot reproduce this.
Defensible SDS statement: below the CMC, SDS is monomer-dominated with dimers bounded ABOVE
by the C12E8 geometric result (≲0.1% at 0.05×CMC rising to ≲0.6% at 0.5×CMC) and pushed
LOWER by charge; trimers an order of magnitude below; n≥4 negligible until the CMC. The
monomer/micelle census above the CMC is robust via the measured CMC, but the exact oligomer
fractions are less determined than for the nonionic — quote them only as bracketed bounds.

BOTTOM LINE
Defensible as physics: >99% monomer below the CMC for both; sub-CMC dimers at the
sub-percent level (C12E8), further suppressed by charge (SDS); both invisible to IFT.
Model-conditional (not measurements): every specific dimer/trimer percentage. Headline
uncertainty is the small-aggregate free-energy functional form, constrained by requiring
the model to reproduce the experimentally sharp CMC.

PROBLEM 3 — Dynamic surface tension of SDS (10^-4, 10^-3, 10^-2 M) at 25 °C
################################################################################

MODEL AND SOURCED PARAMETERS
Diffusion-controlled adsorption via Ward–Tordai:
  Γ(t) = 2·C_b·√(Dt/π) − √(D/π)·∫_0^t C_s(τ)/√(t−τ) dτ
closed with a Langmuir isotherm Γ = Γ∞·K_L·C_s/(1+K_L·C_s) (local sub-surface equilibrium)
and the Szyszkowski–Langmuir equation of state γ = γ0 + RTΓ∞·ln(1−Γ/Γ∞). Solved with an
implicit product-integration scheme.

  Parameter              Value (range)                      Source
  γ0                     72.8 mN/m                          given
  Γ∞                     3.9×10^-6 mol/m² (3.5–4.1)         Tajima 1970 via Prosser & Franses 2001; arXiv 2605.03504
  L_d → K_L              0.037 mm → K_L = 9.49 m³/mol        Tajima 1970 (derived: K_L = L_d/Γ∞); a_L = 0.105 mM
  D (monomer)            5.5×10^-10 m²/s (5.0–8.0)           Javadi et al. 2010 (5.0); micropipette Lee 2017 (5.3±0.3)
  CMC                    8.2 mM                             standard

Three structural caveats stated up front: (i) 10^-2 M is ABOVE the CMC — modeled with free
monomer buffered at C_b = CMC (micelles as reservoir); this is an UPPER bound on relaxation
time (real micellar flux is faster). (ii) The effective-Langmuir EOS overshoots the
surface-pressure magnitude for ionic SDS at high coverage (quantified in Part 4). (iii)
Pure diffusion omits the electrostatic adsorption barrier and any dodecanol impurity.

PARTS 1 & 2 — γ(t) AND Γ(t), WITH UNCERTAINTY
Characteristic diffusion times τ_D = (Γ_eq/C_b)²/D: 0.66 s (10^-4), 0.023 s (10^-3),
4×10^-4 s (10^-2). The dilute case is the SLOWEST and the only one that evolves meaningfully
inside the 0.1–1000 s window.

  t(s)   10^-4 M  Γ(µmol/m²)·γ(mN/m)        10^-3 M  Γ·γ                 10^-2 M  Γ·γ
  0.1    0.69 · 70.9 (70.6–71.0)            3.40 · 53.0 (52.4–53.9)      3.849 · 30.9 (29.3–34.3)
  1      1.38 · 68.6 (68.2–68.7)            3.50 · 50.9 (50.2–52.2)      3.850 · 30.7 (29.0–34.1)
  10     1.75 · 67.1 (66.9–67.2)            3.52 · 50.3 (49.6–51.8)      3.850 · 30.6 (29.0–34.0)
  100    1.85 · 66.6 (66.5–66.7)            3.53 · 50.2 (49.5–51.6)      3.850 · 30.6 (28.9–34.0)
  1000   1.88 · 66.4 (66.3–66.6)            3.53 · 50.1 (49.4–51.6)      3.851 · 30.6 (28.9–34.0)
  (Γ_eq, θ_eq)  1.90 µmol/m², θ=0.49        3.53, θ=0.90                 3.85, θ=0.99

Parenthetical ranges = parameter band (D 5–8×10^-10, Γ∞ 3.5–4.1×10^-6). Physical picture:
only 10^-4 M is genuinely dynamic in this window (θ = 0.18→0.48, γ falls ~4.5 mN/m over
0.1–100 s, then plateaus). 10^-3 M is ~90% equilibrated by 0.1 s (~3 mN/m of evolution).
10^-2 M is fully equilibrated (<1 ms) — flat across the whole window, micelles faster still.

PART 3 — NUMERICAL VALIDATION (three independent checks)
 1. Convergence — refining the mesh 30 → 60 → 120 points/decade changes every tabulated γ
    by <0.05 mN/m. Converged.
 2. Short-time analytic limit Γ(t) → 2C_b√(Dt/π): ratio Γ_num/Γ_analytic = 0.993 → 0.982
    from 10^-4 to 10^-3 s (→1 as t→0). For the near-saturated 10^-2 case the √t law
    correctly breaks (Γ can't exceed Γ_eq) — a physics check, not an error.
 3. Long-time Hansen asymptote (Γ_eq − Γ) ∝ t^(−1/2): fit over 50–5000 s gives
    (Γ_eq−Γ) = 4.62×10^-7·t^(−1/2), R² = 0.99994.

PART 4 — COMPARISON WITH PUBLISHED MEASUREMENTS (honest)
Timescale — AGREEMENT. Brigodiot et al. (2026, arXiv 2608.28112) report that classical
macroscopic (rising-drop) SDS tensiometry gives "almost horizontal" curves that essentially
only capture equilibrium — matching the flat 10^-3/10^-2 predictions. Maximum-bubble-pressure
studies likewise find mM SDS reaching equilibrium within <1 s.
Equilibrium magnitude — PARTIAL DISAGREEMENT (the honest divergence). Measured equilibrium γ
(Wilhelmy, neat water, 25 °C): 10^-4 M ≈ 66–71; 10^-3 M ≈ 56–62; CMC ≈ 38–40 mN/m (Tajima
1970; RSC NJC 2021 d1nj02802b; arXiv 2009.14531). Model: 66.4 / 50.1 / 30.6. So 10^-4 M
agrees (66.4 at the low edge of 66–71); 10^-3 M and CMC are low by ~6–10 mN/m because the
effective-Langmuir EOS omits the positive diffuse-layer (electrostatic) contribution to γ
(~+15 mN/m by Gouy–Chapman; Brigodiot 2026). Honest reading of the table: trust the Γ(t)
column and the relaxation timescale; treat the high-coverage γ values as ~6–9 mN/m too low.
Two regimes where the prediction diverges from real data by construction:
 - Low ionic strength (esp. 10^-4 M): the electrostatic adsorption barrier slows ionic
   adsorption below pure diffusion, so real dilute SDS relaxes somewhat slower — a one-sided
   shift to longer times.
 - Long times with commercial SDS: trace dodecanol causes an extra slow γ decrease over
   10–1000 s and a γ–C minimum (0.02 mol% dodecanol shifts γ by ~20 mN/m;
   par.nsf.gov/10332557). The pure-SDS model excludes this and under-predicts the long-time
   drop of unpurified SDS.
Bottom line: numerically sound (3 checks); Γ(t) and timescales well-sourced and consistent
with the literature; absolute γ at high coverage a known-direction underestimate from the
ionic EOS; real dilute/long-time behavior carries additional electrostatic-barrier and
dodecanol effects not in the model. None of the tabulated γ values are measurements.

PROBLEM 4 — Creaming timeline of a 5 v/v% paraffin-oil-in-water emulsion (d32 = 235 µm)
################################################################################

Paper: Murasiewicz & Illienko, Pol. J. Chem. Tech. 26(1):51–63 (2024), doi:10.2478/pjct-2024-0006.
Decisive point (the paper itself chose speeds "to avoid aeration and prevent creaming during
the experiment," and Fig. 1C shows oil creaming to the top): a stable droplet-size distribution
means NO coalescence; it says nothing about creaming. A 235 µm droplet is enormous, and Stokes
creaming of such droplets takes SECONDS, not months.

SETUP AND RIGID-SPHERE JUSTIFICATION
Oil less dense than water → droplets rise. Hindered Stokes (Richardson–Zaki):
  v = (Δρ·g·d²/18µ_c)·(1−φ)^n.
Stokes (not Hadamard–Rybczynski): the droplet is ~36× more viscous than water (κ = 32/0.89)
and Tween immobilises the interface, so the internal-circulation correction is only +0.9% —
droplets cream as rigid spheres (the paper notes the surfactant makes droplets "act as rigid
spheres").
Properties: water (25 °C) ρ = 997 kg/m³, µ = 0.890 mPa·s; liquid paraffin ρ = 0.85 g/cm³
(Merck 107174, CAS 8042-47-5 — supplier grade matching the paper's 32 mPa·s; range 0.84–0.88);
n_RZ ≈ 4.65.

PART 1 — CREAMING TIMELINE (monodisperse baseline at d32)
Hindered v(d32) = 3.9 mm/s. In a 10 cm column:
 - t50 ≈ 13 s (band 12–16 s, from ρ_oil 0.84–0.875 and n = 4.65–5.5)
 - near-complete separation ≈ 25 s (24–32 s)

  t(s)                        2     5     10    15    20    30    ≥60
  fraction separated (mono)   0.08  0.20  0.39  0.59  0.78  1.00  1.00

Near-linear ramp (serum front rising at constant v) saturating at ~25 s. Final cream layer is
thin (H·φ/φ_cream ≈ 0.1×0.05/0.64 ≈ 8 mm), so ~92% of the column clarifies.

PART 2 — EFFECT OF DISTRIBUTION SHAPE (at fixed d32)
Log-normal DSD, positively skewed; at low rpm the span is broad (paper: >1 at low N). Taking
span ≈ 1.1 (range 0.6–1.3) and mapping to a log-normal volume distribution
(d_v50 = d32·exp(½ln²σ_g)): since v ∝ d² and the volume-median exceeds d32, most oil is in
larger, faster droplets, but a slow small-droplet tail appears.

  case                σ_g    d_v50(µm)   t50      t90     t99
  monodisperse(d32)   —      235         12.8 s   23 s    25 s
  span 0.6            1.26   241         11.1 s   28 s    50 s
  span 1.1            1.51   256         9.2 s    35 s    91 s
  span 1.3            1.61   263         8.5 s    38 s    114 s

Error of the monodisperse approximation (span ≈ 1.1): it OVERestimates t50 by ~40% (13 vs 9 s —
real emulsion starts separating faster) and UNDERestimates the time for 99% separation by ~3.6×
(25 vs 91 s — misses the long tail). It gets the midpoint roughly right but mis-shapes both
ends; error grows with span. Realistic answer: t50 ≈ 9–11 s, near-complete ≈ 1.5–2 min.

PART 3 — COALESCENCE UPPER BOUND, AND WHY IT DOESN'T MOVE THE CURVE
n0 = φ/[(π/6)d32³] = 7.4×10^9 m^-3. Smoluchowski coalescence (dn/dt = −kc n², d32 ∝ n^(−1/3))
+ "no meaningful change" over 30 days (Mastersizer reproducibility ~1%, σ up to ~4%):

  detection threshold on d32    upper bound kc
  <1%                           < 1.6×10^-18 m³/s
  <5%                           < 8.3×10^-18 m³/s
  <10%                          < 1.7×10^-17 m³/s

i.e. a per-droplet coalescence time τ_coal ≳ 100–1000 days (loss rate ≲ 10^-7–10^-8 s^-1).
Interpretation caveat: the barrierless Brownian rate k_Smol = 8kT/3µ = 1.2×10^-17 m³/s gives
only ~7% growth in d32 over a month even at 100% efficiency, so for 235 µm droplets Brownian
collisions are intrinsically negligible and the month-stability only weakly constrains Brownian
efficiency (α < ~0.1–0.7). The physically binding constraint is on the CREAM layer: after ~30 s
all droplets are packed in contact for a month, and an unchanged DSD means the cream-layer
film-rupture time exceeds ~100–1000 days.
How the creaming timeline changes across this bound: IT DOESN'T. Even at the upper bound, d32
grows ~6×10^-5 % over the 30 s creaming time. Creaming (~10–100 s) and coalescence (≳10^7 s)
are separated by ~5–6 orders of magnitude, so the creaming curve is insensitive to coalescence
anywhere in the allowed range. The bound instead governs the fate of the cream (redispersible
packed droplets vs merged free oil) over weeks–months — exactly what the month-long DSD
stability reports.

PART 4 — INPUT PROVENANCE AND FALSIFICATION
Measured (paper): φ = 0.05; d32 = 235 µm (330 rpm, 0.0075 mM Tween 80); log-normal DSD,
positive skew, span ≈ 0.6–1.3; dispersed-phase viscosity 32 mPa·s; DSD unchanged over 30 days;
IFT ≈ 48 mN/m.
Sourced (literature): water ρ = 997 kg/m³, µ = 0.890 mPa·s (25 °C); liquid-paraffin ρ = 0.85
g/cm³ (Merck 107174; range 0.84–0.88) — the single largest uncertainty, ±13% on Δρ hence on
t50; g = 9.81; R–Z n ≈ 4.65; Smoluchowski k = 8kT/3µ.
Assumed: quiescent/isothermal column, no convection/vibration; rigid spherical non-flocculated
droplets; dilute hindrance via R–Z; log-normal span→width mapping; d32 constant during creaming;
Smoluchowski/first-order coalescence kinetics.
What would falsify this: put the FRESH emulsion in a 10 cm column (or the paper's separation
funnel) and track the serum/cream interface vs time — by eye, or better by Turbiscan
(backscattering vs height vs time). Prediction: ~50% clarified in ~10–15 s, ~90% within ~1 min,
with a resolvable slow tail. If separation instead takes hours/days, something is wrong — most
likely droplet flocculation into a space-filling network (not modelled), a far smaller true
droplet size, or a much more viscous continuous phase. The front SHAPE tests Part 2 (sharp →
monodisperse; smeared with a dilute tail → polydisperse). To test Part 3's bound, image the
CREAM layer itself after a month: intact packed droplets confirm the bound; a free-oil layer or
d32 grown ≫10% breaks it.

PROBLEM 5 — Cloud points of CiEj surfactants and PIT of C12E5

THE PREDICTIVE SCHEME — METHOD CONSTANTS vs SYSTEM INPUTS
A single surfactant descriptor, the characteristic curvature Cc, drives both observables.
Method constants (sourced):
 - Cc = 0.28·i + 2.4 − j — group contribution (Ghayour 2017 / Acosta group). Validated: it
   gives Cc(C12E5) = +0.76 vs the independently reported +0.8 (Acosta 2008).
 - Nonionic HLD constants: b = 0.13 (salinity, per g NaCl/100 mL), k = 0.16 (oil),
   cT = +0.06 °C^-1 (temperature; nonionics grow lipophilic as T rises) — Acosta 2008.
 - Cloud-point relation CP = 42.0 − 17.6·Cc — the Cc↔CP linear correlation reported by the
   "Cloud Point of Alkyl Ethoxylates / HLD" work (valid ≤8 EO). Its two coefficients were

   7/31/50/65/78 °C); fit RMSE = 3.1 °C, R² = 0.98. Those five are the sourced calibration data.
 - PIT (HLD = 0): PIT = 25 + (k·EACN − Cc − b·S)/cT.
System inputs: i and j; salinity S; and for PIT the oil EACN (= carbon number for n-alkanes).

(46 vs 50) and C10E6 (56 vs 60) both within ~4 °C.

PART 4 — WHERE THESE PREDICTIONS SHOULD NOT BE TRUSTED
 - j > 8 (very hydrophilic heads): the Cc↔CP correlation is only established for ≤8 EO.
 - Short alkyl chains, i < 10: demonstrated by C8E6 (66 vs 77 °C); constants calibrated on i=10–14.
 - Temperatures far from 25 °C: cT and the linear HLD form are local approximations near 25 °C.
 - Commercial/polydisperse ethoxylates: scheme is for pure monodisperse CiEj.
 - High or non-NaCl salinity: b·S is a linear low-salt NaCl term; fails at high ionic strength /
   specific-ion (Hofmeister) effects.
 - Non-alkane oils: PIT assumes EACN = carbon number; aromatics, esters, polar oils need their own
   (often surfactant-dependent) EACN.
 - Any cosurfactant/alcohol restores the f(A) term, set to zero here.

PROBLEM 6 — ΔG°mic convention dependence (dodecylguanidinium chloride; SDS)
################################################################################

PART 1 — WHICH CONVENTION REPRODUCES −28.3 kJ/mol (dodecylguanidinium chloride = DDGC)
Verified against Bouchal et al., Int. J. Mol. Sci. 2016, 17, 223 (Table 2: CMC = 6.2 mmol/kg,
β = 0.74, ΔmicG° = −28.3 ± 0.9). The paper's printed formula (Eq. 1) is
ΔmicG° = RT(1+β)·ln X_CMC, citing Zana (1996) — but that formula does NOT reproduce its own number.
With CMC = 6.2×10^-3 mol/kg → X_CMC = 1.117×10^-4, RT = 2.4776 kJ/mol (298 K):

  Prefactor used                                        result
  (1+β) = 1.74 — the formula the paper PRINTS            −39.2 kJ/mol
  (2−β) = (1+α) = 1.26 — binding→ionization slot         −28.41 kJ/mol  ← MATCH

The reported −28.3 is reproduced to 0.1 kJ/mol only by the prefactor (2−β) = (1+α) = 1.26
(α = 1−β = 0.26). The (1+β) form the paper writes gives −39.2 — off by ~11 kJ/mol. Confirmed on
the next homologue: TDGC (CMC 1.8 mmol/kg, β 0.72, 306 K) → RT(2−β)·ln X = 1.28×2.544×(−10.34)
= −33.7 vs reported −33.6. So the convention reproducing the published values is the
mole-fraction mass-action expression evaluated with the degree of counter-ion DISSOCIATION
(prefactor 1+α = 2−β), not binding (1+β). The paper's tabulated numbers are internally
inconsistent with its own printed formula — a textbook demonstration of the α↔β convention trap:
the same equation, same measured α/β, gives −28 or −39 depending purely on which symbol goes in
the prefactor.

PART 2 — SDS UNDER EVERY SUBSTANTIABLE CONVENTION
SDS, cmc0 = 0.0083 M, α = 0.272 ± 0.017 (β = 0.728), 298 K. X_cmc = 1.495×10^-4; ln X_cmc = −8.808;
ln(cmc) = −4.792; (2−α) = (1+β) = 1.728.

  #   Convention                                            Formula                         Source                 ΔmicG°
  1   Pseudophase, no counter-ion, MOLARITY (c°=1 M)        RT·ln(cmc)                      Rosen                  −11.9
  2   Pseudophase, no counter-ion, MOLE FRACTION            RT·ln X_cmc                     Zana 1996; Rosen       −21.8
  3   Mass-action + counter-ion, MOLARITY                   (2−α)·RT·ln(cmc)                Zana 1996              −20.5 ± 0.2
  4   Mass-action + counter-ion, MOLE FRACTION              (2−α)·RT·ln X_cmc = (1+β)·...   Zana 1996; Zajac 1997  −37.7 ± 0.4
  5   Full binding limit (β=1), mole fraction               2·RT·ln X_cmc                   Phillips / limiting    −43.6
  (6) α↔β slip variant (as in Part 1)                       (1+α)·RT·ln X_cmc               non-standard; shown    −27.8

(±uncertainties propagate α = ±0.017 through the prefactor; input uncertainty ~±0.4 kJ/mol —
two orders of magnitude below the convention spread.)

PART 3 — SPREAD AND PREDOMINANT USAGE
Total spread across the four standard conventions (#1–4): 25.8 kJ/mol (−11.9 to −37.7);
including the full-binding limit (#5) it widens to ~32 kJ/mol. Input uncertainty from α
(±0.4 kJ/mol) is negligible beside this — the number is dominated almost entirely by convention.
Predominant usage is genuinely split, worsened by inconsistent α/β labelling (some papers use
"β" for dissociation, the opposite of Bouchal): (a) the mole-fraction pseudophase value
RT·ln X_cmc ≈ −21.8 (textbooks, older work), and (b) the mole-fraction mass-action with
counter-ion (2−α)·RT·ln X_cmc ≈ −37.7, standard in rigorous modern ionic-surfactant
thermodynamics (Zana 1996; ITC/conductivity literature, e.g. Bešter-Rogač group). For a
comparable number across careful recent papers, quote #4 (−37.7 kJ/mol); for textbook
tabulations, #2 (−21.8).

PART 4 — RECOMMENDATION (one sentence)
Report ΔmicG° = (2−α)·RT·ln X_cmc (mole-fraction standard state, counter-ion term explicit) while
always stating the numerical value AND definition of α — because mole fraction removes the
arbitrary 1 M standard-state offset, the (2−α) factor is the only form thermodynamically complete
for an ionic surfactant, and spelling out α's meaning forecloses the exact β↔α slip that makes the
same measurement read as −28 or −39 kJ/mol.

Every formula cited to Zana (Langmuir 1996, 12, 1208), Zajac et al. (Langmuir 1997, 13, 1486), or
Rosen's Surfactants and Interfacial Phenomena — none derived here.

PROBLEM 7 — First-principles / semi-empirical CMC of sodium hexadecyl sulfate (STS, C16)
################################################################################
(Chronology preserved: Items 1–2 fixed BEFORE any STS value was consulted; Item 3 is post-prediction.)

ITEM 1 — PREDICTION, FIXED BEFORE ANY STS DATA WAS CONSULTED
Method (semi-empirical, molecular-thermodynamic basis): Klevens' relation,
log10(CMC) = A − B·n_c. Physical content: each CH2 contributes a roughly constant hydrophobic
transfer free energy to ΔG°_mic, so log CMC falls linearly with chain length; B maps to the
per-CH2 free energy (B·2.303·(1+β)·RT ≈ 1.1–1.2 kT per CH2, the known hydrophobic increment)
and A collects headgroup/counterion terms.
Calibration (literature CMCs, 25 °C, pure water; Mukerjee–Mysels / Wikipedia) — C16 deliberately
excluded: C8 0.13 M, C10 0.033 M, C12 0.0082 M, C14 0.0021 M. Fit:
  log CMC = 1.506 − 0.299·n_c  (R² = 0.99998; per-two-carbon factor = 3.96×, matching "÷4 per 2 CH2").
Prediction for C16 (STS): log CMC = 1.506 − 0.299×16 = −3.28 →
  CMC(STS, 25 °C) ≈ 5.3 × 10^-4 M (0.53 mM), realistic range (4–8)×10^-4 M.
The formal fit band is ±1%; the honest ±~30% band comes from the spread between defensible
constant sets (classic Klevens A=1.59, B=0.294 gives 7.7×10^-4) and literature scatter in the
long-chain calibration points, not from the regression itself.

ITEM 2 — CREDIBILITY, ALSO FIXED BEFORE THE STS SEARCH
Leave-one-out cross-validation on the sulfate series (fit the other three, predict the held-out
one — the C14 case is a genuine 2-carbon extrapolation, the exact analog of predicting C16):

  Held-out                                   Predicted   Measured   Error
  C8                                          0.130 M     0.13 M     +0.1 %
  C10                                         0.0327 M    0.033 M    −0.8 %
  C12                                         0.00832 M   0.0082 M   +1.4 %
  C14 (2-C extrapolation ≡ C16 analog)        0.00207 M   0.0021 M   −1.6 %

Every held-out prediction is within ±1.6%. The two-carbon extrapolation mirroring the STS case
errs by only −1.6%, so the METHOD is credible to a few percent; the larger quoted band on STS
reflects input/constant-set uncertainty, not method failure.

ITEM 3 — HELD-OUT COMPARISON (POST-PREDICTION)
Measured literature CMC of sodium hexadecyl sulfate at 25 °C, pure water: standard physical-
chemistry tabulations give CMC = 5.8×10^-4 M with a Krafft point of 45 °C; a second source gives
SDS, SDBS, SHS CMCs as 8.2, 2.7, 0.55 mM (SHS = sodium hexadecyl sulfate = 5.5×10^-4 M).
  Measured ≈ 5.5–5.8 × 10^-4 M.  My prediction was 5.3 × 10^-4 M.
  Honest error: −4% (vs 5.5×10^-4) to −9% (vs 5.8×10^-4), mean ≈ −7% — prediction slightly low
  but squarely inside the stated (4–8)×10^-4 M band and consistent with the ±1.6% method error
  plus a small long-chain curvature.
The measurement also CONFIRMED the key caveat flagged in advance: the Krafft point is 45 °C,
i.e. ABOVE 25 °C.

ITEM 4 — ASSUMPTIONS AND FAILURE MODES
Molecular assumptions: (1) a constant hydrophobic free-energy increment per CH2 (log-linear
Klevens behavior); (2) chain-length-independent headgroup term A and counterion binding β;
(3) the same "normal" globular-micelle geometry across the series; (4) 25 °C, pure water, Na+
counterion, no salt, dilute; (5) a linear, saturated, single alkyl chain.
What makes it fail:
 - Krafft point > 25 °C (decisive here). STS's Krafft temperature is ~45 °C, so at 25 °C the
   hydrated crystal — not micelles — is the equilibrium phase; the surfactant precipitates before
   a micellar solution forms. A literal "dissolve STS at 25 °C and measure the CMC" experiment
   hits the solubility limit first. The Klevens line predicts the WOULD-BE micellization
   concentration, which is exactly why it matches the extrapolated/textbook "25 °C" value rather
   than a direct equilibrium measurement. The number is a metastable/extrapolated CMC, not an
   observable equilibrium one at 25 °C.
 - Long-chain curvature (C18+): slight downward deviation from log-linearity (premicellar
   aggregation / chain folding).
 - Structural scope: branching, unsaturation, aromatic or EO spacers, or a different counterion/
   added salt all shift A (and sometimes B), voiding the sulfate-specific constants.
 - Impurities: hexadecanol from sulfate hydrolysis depresses the apparent CMC.

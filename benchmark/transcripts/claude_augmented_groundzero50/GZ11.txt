What this means

The two approaches disagree on morphology, and the disagreement is physically informative rather than a mistake. This compound is sodium dodecyl sulfate, which in pure water near its CMC is known to form small spherical micelles — so the electrostatic estimate lands on the physically correct answer, while the measured-area calculation over-predicts curvature-reducing packing and lands on rods.

The reason is what each “area” represents. The 44.70 Å² figure is a tightly-packed value (characteristic of a condensed monolayer, or of headgroups whose mutual repulsion has been screened). The electrostatic route instead asks how much area a charged sulfate head actually commands when the double layer is thick: here κ⁻¹ ≈ 34 Å is far larger than the headgroup itself, so unscreened like-charge repulsion swells the effective area to ~67 Å². A bigger head area means a smaller packing parameter and higher interfacial curvature — i.e., spheres.

This also predicts the right trend: adding electrolyte would collapse the double layer, shrink the effective area back toward the packed ~45 Å² value, push the CPP from ~0.31 up through 1/3 into the rodlike regime — which is exactly the salt-induced sphere-to-rod transition SDS is famous for. So the measured-area result isn’t wrong so much as it describes a more screened state than the salt-free CMC condition the problem specifies. The two numbers bracket the surfactant’s behavior across ionic-strength conditions.

(Note: both CPPs sit near the 1/3 boundary — 0.312 just below, 0.470 mid-window — so the morphology is genuinely sensitive to the effective area here, which is why getting the electrostatics right matters for this system.)

Analysis

The system: The compound is sodium bis(2-ethylhexyl) sulfosuccinate — docusate sodium, commonly known as AOT (Aerosol OT). It’s a classic double-tailed anionic surfactant, so the liquid here is an aqueous AOT solution whose surface tension has been driven down to 25.777 mN/m, placed on glass.

Young–Dupré results (from γ_LV = 25.777 mN/m, θ = 24.370°, cos θ ≈ 0.91089):

Work of adhesion: W_A = γ_LV(1 + cos θ) ≈ 49.26 mJ/m²
Spreading coefficient: S = γ_LV(cos θ − 1) ≈ −2.30 mJ/m²

Does it spontaneously wet? No — not in the thermodynamic sense of complete spreading. Spontaneous (complete) wetting requires S ≥ 0, i.e. a contact angle of 0°. Here θ ≈ 24.4° gives S < 0, so the liquid forms a finite droplet rather than spreading into a film.

That said, this is still a strongly wetting-favorable case in the practical sense: the contact angle is well below 90° and the work of adhesion (≈49 mJ/m²) is nearly double the liquid’s own surface tension, meaning the liquid adheres to the glass more strongly than it coheres to itself. It’s “partial wetting” that strongly favors contact — just short of the S ≥ 0 threshold for spontaneous film spreading, which is the criterion the final flag below reports.

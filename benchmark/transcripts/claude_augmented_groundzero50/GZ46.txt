Both values confirmed via the tool. Here’s the assessment from first principles.

The surfactant is sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) — a dodecyl (C₁₂) sulfate head with a Na⁺ counterion. Its strongly negative Cc (−2.63) marks it as quite hydrophilic, which is the key to interpreting the results.

Predicted optimum salinities (S* = exp(k·EACN − Cc), ΔT = 0):

Oil	EACN	Exponent (k·EACN − Cc)	S* (g NaCl eq / 100 mL)
Toluene	0	0 + 2.63 = 2.63	13.87%
Hexadecane	16	2.56 + 2.63 = 5.19	179.47%

Physical achievability: The ceiling here is the solubility of NaCl in water, which saturates at roughly 26 g/100 mL (~26% w/v) at room temperature. You cannot make a brine more concentrated than that.

Toluene (13.87%) sits comfortably below saturation — about half the maximum. This is readily achievable in practice; a ~14% NaCl brine is an ordinary working concentration.
Hexadecane (179.47%) is roughly 7× the saturation limit. It is physically impossible to prepare such a brine — the salt would precipitate long before you got there. So SDS alone cannot reach the Winsor III balanced state with hexadecane by salinity tuning.

This is chemically sensible rather than a quirk of the numbers: SDS is a hydrophilic ionic surfactant, and balancing it against a very lipophilic oil (long n-alkane) would require screening the sulfate headgroup charge far more than attainable salinity allows. In real EOR/formulation practice you’d reach for a more lipophilic (less negative Cc) surfactant, or add a lipophilic co-surfactant/co-solvent, rather than pushing salinity.

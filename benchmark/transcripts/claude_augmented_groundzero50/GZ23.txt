Valid HLB method: strictly, none yields a trustworthy number for this structure. Working through the two candidate methods:

Davies’ group-contribution method is structurally invalid for geminis. It’s group-additive, which assumes each fragment contributes as it would in a simple monomeric surfactant. A gemini spacer doesn’t pack or contribute like a free lipophilic tail, so no additive bookkeeping captures its real effect. This is a structural failure, not a missing-group-number problem.
Griffin’s mass-ratio method is the better-justified method name, but it cannot be trusted numerically here. Griffin (HLB = 20 × M_hydrophilic/M_total) assumes hydrophilicity scales with the mass of the polar portion. That holds for nonionics (its entire validated evidence base, including the published aminoglycerol gemini series, is nonionic). For a strongly ionic head, hydrophilicity is dominated by charge, not polar mass — and there’s a genuine bookkeeping ambiguity about whether the Br⁻ counterions count as “hydrophilic mass,” which has been shown to swing the computed HLB by roughly 2× for exactly a cationic 12-4-12 gemini.

A trap worth naming explicitly: the literature “12-4-12” HLB value of ~6.2 (Liao et al. 2023) is for a 1-dodecylaminoglycerol gemini — a nonionic compound that merely shares the C12/C4/C12 shorthand. It is a different headgroup chemistry and does not transfer to this cationic bis-quat.

So the defensible conclusion: HLB as a framework was built for nonionics; for this permanently-charged gemini, Griffin is the least-unjustified method to name but should not be used to report an exact value, and Davies is outright inappropriate. For real hydrophilic/lipophilic characterization of this surfactant you’d want an ionic-appropriate descriptor (e.g., HLD / characteristic curvature) rather than a classical HLB number.

The fitted pre-CMC curve typically stops matching the measured data well in the high-concentration region immediately approaching and at the Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC).
(b) Physical Explanation for the Divergence
Pre-CMC adsorption isotherm models (such as the Langmuir, Frumkin, or Szyszkowski equations) are derived under the assumption that all surfactant added to the system exists as individual monomers in the bulk solution. These models assume that as the total concentration increases, the monomer concentration increases continuously, leading to a steady increase in surface adsorption and a continuous decrease in surface tension.
However, in real systems, as the concentration approaches the CMC, two major physical phenomena occur that the pre-CMC models fail to account for:
Micellization (The Primary Reason): At the CMC, surfactant molecules spontaneously self-assemble into micelles in the bulk phase. This aggregation effectively caps the chemical potential and the concentration of free monomers in the solution. Since the surface tension is strictly governed by the monomer concentration at the air-water interface, the measured surface tension abruptly levels off into a plateau. The pre-CMC model, lacking a thermodynamic term for micelle formation, continues to predict a decreasing surface tension (or a different asymptote) and thus diverges sharply from the measured plateau.
The "Dip" Effect (Impurities): In many real-world measurements, highly surface-active trace impurities (such as long-chain alcohols) accumulate at the interface just below the CMC, causing a local minimum or "dip" in the surface tension curve. Once the CMC is reached, these impurities are solubilized into the newly formed micelles, causing the surface tension to rise back to the value expected for the pure surfactant. Pure pre-CMC models for the bulk surfactant cannot predict this localized dip-and-rise behavior.

This model combines the Langmuir adsorption isotherm with the ideal 2D gas equation of state, yielding the relationship:

$$\pi = \gamma_0 - \gamma = A \ln(1 + KC)$$

where $\pi$ is the surface pressure, $A = RT\Gamma_{max}$ is a constant related to the maximum surface excess, and $K$ is the adsorption equilibrium constant.

### 1. Analysis of the Equation of State (Gibbs Adsorption Isotherm)
Using the Gibbs adsorption equation for a nonionic surfactant, the surface excess concentration $\Gamma$ is related to the slope of the surface pressure with respect to the natural logarithm of concentration:
$$\Gamma = \frac{1}{RT} \frac{d\pi}{d\ln C}$$

By calculating the local slopes ($\Delta \pi / \Delta \ln C$) between the data points, we observe that the slope (and thus $\Gamma$) rises quickly at very low concentrations and then remains remarkably constant (approx. 7.4 mN/m, or $\Gamma \approx 3.0\ \mu\text{mol/m}^2$) across the majority of the concentration range (0.20 mM to 1.90 mM).

In an ideal 2D gas model (the basis of the von Szyszkowski equation), the surface pressure is defined as $\pi = -RT\Gamma_{max} \ln(1 - \theta)$, where $\theta = \Gamma/\Gamma_{max}$. If $\Gamma$ is nearly constant (the surface is saturated), $\pi$ should also plateau and remain nearly constant. However, the data shows that $\pi$ continues to increase linearly with $\ln C$ even while $\Gamma$ is saturated. This discrepancy indicates that the adsorbed layer does not behave as an ideal 2D gas; the molecules are exerting a higher 2D pressure than predicted by independent adsorption, which is a hallmark of non-ideal lateral interactions (specifically, steric/hard-disk repulsion described by models like Volmer or Frumkin).

### 2. Testing the von Szyszkowski Fit
If we attempt to fit the 2-parameter von Szyszkowski equation to the 7 high-precision data points (provided to three decimal places), we encounter a fundamental contradiction:
*   **To fit the high-concentration data** (where the slope is $\approx 7.4$), the parameter $A$ must be approximately 7.4. *   **To fit the low-concentration data**, we must use a smaller $K$ and a larger $A$ (e.g., $A \approx 9.4, K \approx 15$). While this matches the endpoints, it creates a systematic "U-shaped" residual pattern, under-predicting the surface pressure in the intermediate range (0.10 to 0.80 mM) by more than 2.0 mN/m. 

In high-precision tensiometry, a systematic deviation of >2 mN/m is physically significant and far exceeds experimental noise. This systematic failure proves that a simple independent adsorption model is mathematically and physically insufficient to describe the system.

### Conclusion
The surfactant molecules do not adsorb independently. The interface exhibits significant non-ideality, requiring an interaction parameter (such as the Frumkin interaction parameter $a$ or Volmer co-area) to account for the intermolecular forces at the interface.

To determine the critical micelle concentration (CMC) and the counterion binding degree ($\beta$) from the specific conductivity data, we apply the standard electrokinetic model for ionic surfactants (often associated with the Evans equation or the Onsager limiting law for micellar solutions).

### 1. Analysis of the Conductivity Data
The specific conductivity ($\kappa$) of an ionic surfactant solution increases linearly with concentration ($C$) in two distinct regimes:
1.  **Pre-micellar region (monomers):** The slope ($S_1$) represents the molar conductivity of the fully dissociated surfactant monomers.
2.  **Post-micellar region (micelles + monomers):** The slope ($S_2$) is lower because the micelles have a lower charge density and mobility, and a fraction of the counterions are "bound" to the micelle surface, reducing the number of charge carriers.

We can determine the slopes by performing linear regressions on the linear portions of the data:

**Pre-micellar region ($C = 1.0$ to $5.0$ mM):**
Using the data points $(1.0, 0.692)$, $(2.0, 1.327)$, $(3.0, 1.974)$, $(4.0, 2.605)$, and $(5.0, 3.260)$:
*   The change in conductivity $\Delta\kappa$ over $\Delta C = 4.0$ mM is $3.260 - 0.692 = 2.568$ mS/cm.
*   Slope $S_1 \approx 2.568 / 4.0 = 0.642$ mS·cm²/mmol.
*   A precise linear regression yields: $\kappa = 0.6414 C + 0.0474$.

**Post-micellar region ($C = 7.0$ to $12.0$ mM):**
Using the data points $(7.0, 4.191)$, $(8.0, 4.477)$, $(10.0, 5.072)$, and $(12.0, 5.659)$:
*   The change in conductivity $\Delta\kappa$ over $\Delta C = 5.0$ mM is $5.659 - 4.191 = 1.468$ mS/cm.
*   Slope $S_2 \approx 1.468 / 5.0 = 0.2936$ mS·cm²/mmol.

ABSTRACT
Dynamic surface tension and interfacial dilational measurements are used to assign adsorption, diffusion, and structural- 
relaxation mechanisms in surfactant systems. However, small unmeasured variations in area forcing or baseline stability can 
generate identifiable relaxation modes. We quantify this failure mode, termed protocol-  induced false identifiability, using a 
nested entropy-  constrained hierarchy for soluble nonionic surfactant interfaces. Model M0 describes diffusion with instanta -
neous interfacial equilibrium, Model M1 adds finite-  rate adsorption, and Model M2 adds a nonequilibrium internal variable. 
Conservation, nonnegative entropy production, free-  energy decay, numerical convergence, and nested limits were verified. With 
multi- protocol forcing, synthetic estimation recovered the four M2 dynamic parameters with median absolute relative errors 
of 0.34%–2.02%, showing that the model is identifiable in principle. Experimental falsification used two independent digitiza -
tions of a published polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl ether step-  relaxation trace, yielding 148 consensus points. Under an equal-  step 
assumption, M2 improved the corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) by 36.61 relative to M0; however, diffusion-  only 
models with linear drift or segment-  specific amplitudes outperformed M2 by 5.68 and 16.84 AICc units, respectively. M2 also 
increased leave-  one- segment- out error by 8.95%, whereas the drift model reduced it by 35.6%. Published oscillatory spectra 
provided additional- mode screening: Poloxamer P9400 showed the strongest signal, but available records were insufficient for 
locked nonlinear validation. Because the commercial ethoxylate and block- copolymer datasets lack batch- resolved compositional 
characterization sufficient for microscopic attribution, their reanalysis is restricted to model-  discrimination screening rather 
than compound- specific mechanism assignment. The audit offers a workflow for preventing false mechanistic assignments by 
requiring competition with nuisance-  protocol models and locked cross- protocol prediction.
1   |   Introduction
Fluid interfaces containing surface-  active species evolve 
through coupled bulk transport, adsorption and desorption, mo-
lecular rearrangement, and interfacial deformation. These pro -
cesses determine the performance of foams, emulsions, coatings, 
detergents, pharmaceutical dispersions, enhanced-  oil- recovery 
fluids, and microfluidic systems. Equilibrium surface tension 
alone is inadequate when interface creation or deformation 
occurs on timescales comparable to surfactant transport and 
interfacial equilibration (Ward and Tordai  1946; Bergfreund 
et al. 2021; Kovalchuk et al. 2023). Dynamic surface tension, γ(t), 
and the complex interfacial dilational modulus then provide the 
principal experimental records of the evolving interfacial layer.

The classical diffusion-  controlled description is rooted in the 
Ward–Tordai formulation (Ward and Tordai  1946). Subsequent 
theories have introduced finite adsorption barriers, nonideal 
equations of state, electrostatic interactions, curvature, micel -
lization, multicomponent transport, and internal interfacial 
states. Recent microscopic and experimentally constrained 
studies have improved forward descriptions of adsorption 
kinetics over wide concentration ranges (Scher et  al.  2023; 
Huang et  al.  2025), while an entropy- regulated tensiometric 
treatment coupled adsorption kinetics with high-  precision 
dynamic surface-  tension measurements (Al-  Sou'od  2026). 
Kovalchuk et  al.  ( 2023) emphasized that quantitative inter -
pretation of surfactant layers should connect tensiometry with 
thermodynamic equations of state, direct information on in -
terfacial composition, structural characterization, and molec -
ular modeling; dilational rheology is particularly sensitive to 
small changes in composition and structure. These advances 
sharpen forward modeling, but forward adequacy does not 
by itself establish that a mechanism or fitted parameter is 
uniquely identified by a measured response.
Sample composition is a separate and potentially domi -
nant source of ambiguity. Classical work by Mysels and 
Florence ( 1973) and Lunkenheimer and Miller ( 1987) showed 
that trace surface-  active impurities can strongly alter dy -
namic or equilibrium interfacial behavior, and Priester 
et al. ( 1998) demonstrated this specifically for oligoethylene-  
glycol ether surfactants. Commercial ethoxylates and PEO-  
PPO- PEO block copolymers can additionally contain homolog 
and molecular-  mass distributions. Consequently, a relaxation 
feature observed in a commercial material cannot be assigned 
uniquely to a molecular adsorption or rearrangement mecha -
nism unless composition and surface-  chemical purity are in -
dependently established.
Mechanistic ambiguity and model dependence are therefore not 
new problems. Boos et al. (2013) showed that quantitative differ-
ences among interfacial-  rheology data and analysis routes can 
alter interpretation even for carefully studied nonionic systems. 
Rosales- Anzola and Urbina- Villalba (2025) further showed that 
agreement with equilibrium surface tension is necessary but 
not sufficient for selecting an equation of state and adsorption 
isotherm, and Rosales- Anzola (2025) demonstrated that the pre-
dictive fidelity of multicomponent dilational models depends 
critically on the thermodynamic adsorption model. Free-  energy 
formulations of adsorption kinetics likewise provide alternative 
nonequilibrium constructions and clarify the limits of equilib -
rium mappings between coverage and tension (Diamant and 
Andelman  1996). The present study therefore does not claim 
first recognition of mechanistic non- uniqueness; it isolates and 
quantifies one narrower failure mode: apparent mechanistic 
identifiability created by fixing uncertain protocol variables to 
ideal values.
A dynamic surface-  tension or dilational record is not a direct 
measurement of one elementary process. Bulk diffusion, inter -
facial transfer, micelle disassembly, lateral interactions, mo -
lecular orientation, aggregation, and structural relaxation can 
generate overlapping signatures. Different parameter combina -
tions can therefore produce nearly indistinguishable responses. 
A visually excellent fit to one record neither proves the assumed 
mechanism nor ensures that fitted diffusion coefficients, kinetic 
constants, or relaxation times will transfer to another concentra-
tion, area history, or forcing frequency.
The ambiguity is especially important when the adsorbed layer 
possesses its own structural dynamics. Conventional mod -
els assume
so that every instantaneous surface excess, Γ, maps to a 
unique surface tension. Experimental studies instead show 
concentration-   and frequency-  dependent responses associated 
with molecular orientation, packing, association, and lateral 
organization (Bergfreund et al.  2021; Kovalchuk et al.  2023 , 
2024 ; Chen et al. 2024). Generalized interfacial rheology mod -
els can represent broad response spectra accurately (Loglio 
et al.  2024), but numerical flexibility does not prove that fit -
ted coefficients correspond uniquely to distinct molecular 
processes.
The minimal thermodynamic extension is therefore to introduce 
an internal variable, 𝜉, and enlarge the interfacial state space to
Interfaces with the same Γ may then exhibit different tensions 
because their molecular organization and history differ. This 
construction permits delayed relaxation, aging, overshoot, and 
compression–expansion hysteresis while separating the amount 
adsorbed from the state of interfacial organization.
Any enlarged state space must satisfy the second law. 
Adsorption and structural relaxation must be driven by conju -
gate thermodynamic forces and constrained so that interfacial 
entropy production remains nonnegative (van Sluijs et al. 2025). 
Thermodynamic admissibility, however, is only a necessary 
condition. Structural identifiability asks whether ideal observa -
tions uniquely determine the parameters of a specified model, 
whereas practical identifiability asks whether finite, noisy ob -
servations determine them with bounded uncertainty (Wieland 
et  al. 2021; Simpson and Maclaren  2023). Identifiability anal-
ysis can also determine which perturbations are minimally 
sufficient to distinguish competing mechanisms (Gevertz and 
Kareva 2024 ).
This need for mechanism discrimination is especially relevant 
to polymer- surfactant and other multiscale interfacial systems, 
in which transport and relaxation processes can contribute si -
multaneously to the measured dynamic surface tension (Ritacco 
et al. 2025).
The present work asks a narrow and falsifiable question: con -
ditional on a specified constitutive family, can a thermody -
namically admissible additional interfacial relaxation mode 
be distinguished from small, unmeasured variations in the 
experimental forcing? We construct a nested model hierarchy 
comprising M0, M1, and M2 from a common free-  energy struc-
ture; verify conservation, dissipation, numerical convergence, 
and limiting relations; establish coefficient-  rank conditions 
and multi- protocol synthetic recoverability; and require candi -
date mechanisms to compete with finite-  adsorption, drift, and

2.2   |   Interfacial Free Energy and Surface Tension
The M2 construction is deliberately phenomenological and 
minimal. It follows a free-  energy-  based nonequilibrium strat -
egy (Diamant and Andelman  1996; van Sluijs et  al.  2025) 
rather than postulating a specific molecular rearrangement 
pathway. The variable ξ is a macroscopic internal coordinate 
that permits the interfacial state to retain one delayed degree 
of freedom beyond surface excess; it is not identified a priori 
with orientation, aggregation, packing, or conformational 
change.

The coverage-  dependent terms provide a Frumkin-  type inter -

penalty is chosen so that the nonequilibrium contribution van -

value. This is one admissible constitutive choice for stress- 
testing an additional relaxation mode; it is not asserted to be a 
universal equation of state for surfactant layers.

Accordingly, the equilibrium constitutive component of the hi -

constitutive family being tested.

3   |   Materials and Methods
3.1   |   Numerical Solution and Verification
The diffusion equation was discretized conservatively on a 
stretched finite-  volume grid refined near the interface. The ki -
netic boundary relation was eliminated analytically using the 
Wright omega function, avoiding a nested nonlinear root solve 
and maintaining positive subsurface concentration. The result -
ing stiff method-  of- lines system was integrated with an implicit 
backward- differentiation method.
Every model was subjected to four non-  negotiable checks: total 
mass balance, nonnegative entropy production, nonincreasing 
grand potential under closed isothermal conditions, and conver-
gence with mesh and domain refinement. Nested limiting rela -
tions were also tested directly: M1 approached M0 as DaΓ → ∞, 
and M2 approached M1 as 𝜅 → 0 or as structural relaxation be -
came instantaneous.
3.2   |   Synthetic Recovery and Protocol Design
The synthetic parameter values were illustrative rather than 
experimental estimates. M0 recovery used 100 noisy repli -
cates. M1 used expansion and compression responses with 
50 replicates. M2 used expansion and compression step re -
sponses together with a complex frequency response, followed 
by prediction of a held-  out mild-  expansion protocol over 30 
noise realizations. Positive parameters were estimated on a 
logarithmic scale. Practical identifiability was assessed using

3.3   |   Published Experimental Datasets
This study combines original model development, numerical 
verification, synthetic recovery, and reanalysis of two pub -
lished experimental datasets (Hansen  2008 ; Loglio et al. 1995); 
no new experiments were performed. The DC12PO dataset 
served as an external diffusion-  controlled comparator and 
contained a digitized transient area-  pulse response plus seven 
harmonic conditions from the same sample. Hansen's study 
provided oscillatory curves for polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl 
ether, Poloxamer 188, and Poloxamer P9400, together with 
step- relaxation traces or parameter summaries. Coordinates 
extracted from published figures are explicitly treated as 
digitized observations and are never represented as original 
instrument data. The datasets and their diagnostic roles are 
summarized in Table 1 .
Material identities are reported using generic names, with 
the corresponding commercial trade names given here 
only for source identification: dodecanol poly(oxyeth -
ylene)23 (C12EO23; polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl ether; Brij 
35), EO76- PO30-  EO76 (Poloxamer 188; Pluronic F68), and 
EO21- PO50-  EO21 (Poloxamer P9400; Pluronic P9400) 
(Hansen  2008). Thereafter, the generic names polyoxyeth -
ylene (23) lauryl ether, Poloxamer 188, and Poloxamer P9400 
are used throughout the manuscript.
Polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl ether, Poloxamer 188, and 
Poloxamer P9400 are commercially designated surfactant ma -
terials and are not treated here as chemically monodisperse 
model compounds. The nominal compositions are retained 
only to identify the systems reported in the source study. The 
published records available for the present reanalysis do not 
provide batch-  resolved homolog distributions, molecular-  
mass distributions, trace surface-  active impurity profiles, or 
other compositional information sufficient to reconstruct the 
surface-  chemical purity of the tested samples. Such effects 
are known to modify interfacial adsorption and relaxation 
behavior (Mysels and Florence  1973; Lunkenheimer and 
Miller  1987; Priester et al.  1998). Accordingly, these datasets 
are used strictly as diagnostic records for model discrimina -
tion and protocol sensitivity, not for assigning compound-  
specific microscopic adsorption mechanisms.
The concentration choices were fixed from source-  level diag -
nostic roles before model comparison. The C12EO23 dataset 
(polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl ether, 2 ppm) was selected because 
Hansen described the low-  pressure surface as almost entirely 
elastic and well represented by diffusional exchange. The 
EO76- PO30-  EO76 dataset (Poloxamer 188, 10,000 ppm) was 
selected because it had the smallest published step-  relaxation 
fitting error for that block copolymer. The EO21- PO50-  EO21 
dataset (Poloxamer P9400, 10 ppm) was selected because it 
was below the reported critical micelle concentration (CMC), 
provided a complete resolvable E′/E″ spectrum, and exhibited 
source-  reported frequency dependence attributed to config -
urational relaxation. These prespecified diagnostic rules re -
duce, but do not eliminate, selection bias; analysis across all 
published concentrations remains a desirable extension.
These prespecified roles are logical roles in the falsification de -
sign, not molecular positive or negative controls. They reduce 
post hoc selection of datasets but do not remove uncertainty 
due to commercial composition, sample history, or unreported 
impurities.
For digitized Hansen spectra, conservative uncertainties were 
assigned as 0.15/0.25 mN m−1 for polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl

3.6   |   Model Selection and Claim Rules
Comparisons used corrected Akaike information criterion 
(AICc), leave-  one- frequency- out or leave-  one- segment- out 
prediction, profile likelihood, parametric bootstrap for the 
boundary case, and Monte Carlo propagation of digitization un -
certainty. Parsimony was imposed as an inference rule rather 
than a qualitative preference: a more complex model was re -
tained only when its additional parameters were practically 
bounded, its information-  criterion advantage was stable, held- 
out prediction improved, and neither a simpler physical model 
nor a plausible nuisance-  protocol model provided an equally 
adequate explanation. When those requirements were not met, 
the simpler model was retained. Even when a reduced spectral 
mode passed these tests, it was classified only as screening ev -
idence rather than validation of the nonlinear M2 mechanism. 
AICc was interpreted only within the candidate set being com -
pared; decisive preference over an underspecified baseline was 
not treated as mechanistic evidence. When the required equi -
librium and adsorption observables are available, constitutive 
alternatives must likewise enter the candidate set before a mo -
lecular mechanism is assigned.

5   |   Discussion
5.1   |   From Thermodynamic Admissibility to a 
Defensible Mechanism
The model hierarchy establishes what can be represented con -
sistently, not what a finite experimental record can uniquely 
prove. M2 is thermodynamically admissible, satisfies the re -

degenerate conditions. The synthetic experiments further show 
that its dynamic parameters can be recovered when the forcing 
histories are known and sufficiently diverse. These results are 
necessary validation of the framework, but they are conditional 
on the information supplied to the inverse problem.

5.2   |   Protocol- Induced False Identifiability as 
the Central Finding
Protocol- induced false identifiability is defined here as an ap -
parently bounded and strongly selected mechanistic parame -
ter whose support depends on fixing uncertain features of the 
forcing protocol to ideal values. The polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl 
ether transient provides the direct demonstration. Within the 
restricted M0–M2 hierarchy, M2 improved AICc by 36.61 units 
and produced a nominal structural amplitude and relaxation 
time. Once small baseline drift or segment-  dependent step am-
plitudes were admitted, however, diffusion-  only nuisance mod-
els became more parsimonious than M2.
The reversal was not merely an information-  criterion effect. M2 
reduced the full- record residual but increased leave- one- segment- 
out error by 8.95% relative to M0, whereas the drift model reduced 
that error by 35.6%. Thus the added structural coordinate interpo-
lated residual structure without transferring as a reproducible ma-
terial property. A bounded estimate obtained under the equal- step 
assumption was therefore conditional, not mechanistically secure.
This conclusion does not assert that the original experiment con-
tained instrumental drift or incorrect area steps. It identifies an 
information limit in the published record. The fitted nuisance 
magnitudes were small—a baseline change of approximately 
0.058 mN m−1 over 80 s and only a 3.39% span in segment ampli-
tudes—yet they occupied the same residual scale as the nominal 
structural contribution. Without the measured area waveform 
and baseline history, the tension trace alone cannot distinguish 
those explanations.
5.3   |   Why AICc, Bounded Profiles, and Good Fits 
Are Insufficient
AICc ranks models only within the candidate set supplied to 
it. The large preference for M2 over M0 was mathematically 
correct for the restricted comparison and scientifically incom -
plete because the model universe omitted lower-  dimensional 
protocol explanations. Profile likelihood is subject to the same 
conditionality: a parameter may be bounded within one model 
while disappearing when an omitted nuisance degree of free -
dom is introduced.
Held- out transfer is therefore the sharper discriminator. A 
mechanistic extension is retained only when thermodynamic 
admissibility, practical parameter bounds, superiority over sim -
pler physical and nuisance models, and improved prediction 
of data not used for estimation are all satisfied. This is the op -
erational parsimony rule used here: added parameters are not 
rewarded for reducing residuals alone. If predictive adequacy 
is comparable, the lower-  complexity explanation is preferred. 
The collapse of the finite-  barrier model toward M0 removes 
one physical alternative in the Hansen spectra, but it does not 
remove protocol confounding, composition/purity uncertainty, 
or constitutive-  model uncertainty. Candidate-  set completeness 
and transfer testing are separate requirements, and neither can 
substitute for the other.

5.4   |   Scope of the Cross- System Evidence
The four experimental systems support a graded rather than 
binary interpretation. DC12PO rejects the additional mode 
through failure of cross-  domain transfer. The polyoxyethylene 
(23) lauryl ether raw trace demonstrates loss of mechanistic 
preference after nuisance controls. Poloxamer 188 remains in -
termediate because its additional-  mode amplitude is bounded 
but its relaxation timescale is broad. Poloxamer P9400 provides 
the strongest spectral screening evidence that a single diffusion 
mode is insufficient: the structural surrogate improves AICc, 
leave- one- frequency- out prediction, and robustness to digitiza -
tion uncertainty, whereas the finite adsorption-  barrier model 
does not.
These outcomes do not establish that the same nonlinear M2 
mechanism operates in any of the experimental systems. In par -
ticular, Poloxamer P9400 cannot be treated as a validated M2 
case because the raw step-  relaxation trace and measured area 
history required for locked time-  domain testing were unavail -
able. The defensible conclusion is narrower: within the tested 
reduced hierarchy, the Poloxamer P9400 spectrum supports an 
additional positive relaxation mode whose microscopic origin 
and nonlinear state-  space interpretation remain unresolved. 
The conclusion is conditional on the reduced constitutive family 
and on the incompletely characterized composition of the com -
mercial material.
The internal variable must therefore remain a macroscopic la -
tent coordinate. It represents a nonequilibrium contribution that 
may be required after diffusion, finite-  rate adsorption, and plau-
sible protocol explanations have been excluded. It is not direct 
evidence for a unique molecular orientation, aggregation state, 
packing transition, or conformational pathway. Molecular inter-
pretation requires independent structural observables or experi -
mentally locked multi- protocol validation.
An additional limitation applies specifically to polyoxyeth -
ylene (23) lauryl ether, Poloxamer 188, and Poloxamer P9400. 
Their detailed homolog or molecular-  mass distributions, trace 
surface- active impurities, and batch-  specific sample histories 
cannot be reconstructed from the published records. Such com -
positional factors may generate or modify relaxation signatures 
independently of the protocol effects analyzed here (Mysels 
and Florence  1973; Lunkenheimer and Miller  1987; Priester 
et  al.  1998). They are therefore unresolved confounders, not 
mechanisms that the present reanalysis can identify or exclude.
5.5   |   Minimum Evidential Standard for Future 
Experiments
A defensible surfactant dynamic-  interface experiment must 
characterize the material and record the forcing history with 
fidelity comparable to the response. Sample identity, supplier/
batch, relevant purity or homolog- distribution information, con-
centration preparation, and sample history should be retained 
whenever they can affect interfacial behavior. For every per -
turbation, the measured area waveform A (t), rise time, baseline 
evolution, instrument response, and replicate identity should be 
retained together with the surface- tension record γ(t). Nominally 
repeated steps are insufficient; deliberately varied amplitudes, 
durations, and frequencies are needed to decorrelate material 
relaxation from protocol imperfections.
The inference plan should be fixed at the model-  class level 
before interpreting the fitted mechanism. M0–M2 should 
compete against drift, offset, amplitude asymmetry, and 
instrument-  response alternatives, while plausible equations 
of state or adsorption isotherms should also be challenged 
when the equilibrium surface-  tension, surface-  excess, and 
concentration-  resolved data required to constrain them are 
available. At least one protocol class should be reserved for 
locked prediction, while profile likelihood, bootstrap uncer -
tainty, leave-  one- segment- out, leave-  one- frequency- out, and 
time- to- frequency transfer should be treated as primary evi -
dence. An internal mode should be assigned only if it survives 
these tests with shared parameters and is not an artifact of 
sample composition or model form.
5.6   |   Boundaries of the Present Evidence 
and Falsifiable Next Tests
The present evidence is limited by raster-  digitized coordinates, 
unavailable original instrument files, the absence of the mea -
sured area history in figure  5 of Hansen (Hansen  2008), in -
complete composition/purity information for the commercial 
Hansen materials, and constitutive-  model uncertainty. The 
two- pass consensus constrains extraction error but cannot re -
cover pixel-  level covariance, undocumented preprocessing, 
the true segment-  wise forcing, or batch-  specific surface-  active 
contaminants. The reduced frequency hierarchy screens among 
diffusion, a finite adsorption barrier, and one additional posi -
tive mode; it does not identify the complete nonlinear M2 pa -
rameter vector and cannot discriminate among alternative 
adsorption equations of state without independent equilibrium 
adsorption observables. These limitations can be as important 
as, or more important than, the protocol uncertainty quanti -
fied here; the present study does not rank their universal mag -
nitudes. The selected datasets test logical falsification roles 
rather than population-  level generality across surfactants and 
concentrations.
These limitations define direct falsification experiments. 
polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl ether should be repeated with a 
compositionally characterized sample, synchronized A (t) and 
γ(t), deliberately varied step amplitudes, and an independently 
characterized baseline. Poloxamer P9400 requires raw step-  
relaxation records, measured area histories, and sufficient 
compositional characterization so that one parameter set 
can be locked across time-  and frequency-  domain protocols. 
Equilibrium surface tension and, where feasible, independent 
surface-  excess data are also needed to test alternative adsorp -
tion equations of state before interpreting a dynamic mode 
molecularly. Extension across concentrations and chemi -
cally distinct, well-  characterized surfactants should then test 
whether the graded evidence pattern persists. Only if an added 
mode survives composition controls, constitutive-  model com -
petition, nuisance-  protocol competition, and prospective 
transfer should it be promoted from a useful surrogate to a 
validated interfacial mechanism.

6   |   Conclusions
An entropy-  constrained internal-  variable model can be ther -
modynamically admissible and identifiable under known, suf -
ficiently diverse perturbations. Synthetic calculations verified 
conservation, dissipation, nested limits, and accurate recovery 
of the four M2 dynamic parameters. This establishes identifi -
ability of the specified model under informative protocols; it 
does not establish M2 as a universal constitutive description. 
The experimental analysis exposed the harder problem: an ad -
ditional relaxation mode can appear decisively selected when 
unmeasured protocol features are fixed to ideal values.
Two- pass digitization of the polyoxyethylene (23) lauryl ether 
transient independently recovered the published diffusion rate. 
Under equal-  step assumptions, M2 improved AICc by 36.61, 
but diffusion-  only models with small drift or step-  amplitude 
variation were more parsimonious, and M2 performed worse 
in leave-  one- segment- out prediction. This is direct evidence of 
protocol- induced false identifiability, not evidence for a poly -
oxyethylene (23) lauryl ether structural mode.
Poloxamer P9400 provides the strongest additional-  mode 
screening signal, Poloxamer 188 remains intermediate, and 
DC12PO retains M0. None of these experimental results val -
idates the nonlinear M2 mechanism or a unique microscopic 
relaxation pathway. The commercial-  material results are ad -
ditionally conditional on unresolved composition/purity and 
on the reduced constitutive family. For surfactant tensiometry 
and interfacial rheology, the proposed audit supplies one layer 
of a broader validation sequence: characterize the material and 
equilibrium layer, challenge plausible constitutive models, com-
pare mechanistic and nuisance-  protocol alternatives, require 
bounded parameters, and reserve at least one protocol for locked 
prediction. An internal interfacial mode should be assigned only 
after these independent sources of uncertainty have been chal -
lenged successfully.

Abstract
Bridging microscopic surfactant thermodynamics with macroscopic continuum fluid dynamics  
requires  a  structured  computational  infrastructure,  precise  experimental  validation  datasets,  and  an 
efficient division of labor between specialized artificial intelligence agents. In this operational roadmap,  
we delineate the complete technical requirements and execution blueprint for the Surfactant-Dynamics-
Sim project across four sequential phases. First, we outline the exact data mining requirements, focusing  
on four primary classes of experimental records: dynamic surface tensiometry (DST), large-amplitude  
oscillatory dilatational (LAOD) interfacial rheology, microfluidic droplet pinch-off timeseries, and thin  
liquid film foam drainage isotherms. Second, hardware and software environments are comprehensively  
specified, detailing the utilization of local Intel Core Ultra 7 CPUs, 8 GB Intel Arc 140V GPUs (for  
neural operator surrogate acceleration), remote Kaggle GPU environments (for heavy coarse-grained  
molecular dynamics and Bayesian sampling), and the pre-installed Scilab-Xcos 2025 numerical engine.  
Third, an operational collaboration framework is established between Antigravity (the local execution  
and numerical agent) and Arena AI (the browser-based data mining and literature extraction agent),  
synchronized  through  GitHub  (prashant-kotian/surfactant-dynamics-sim)  and  Google  Drive  
(kotianinterface@gmail.com).  Finally,  a  rigorous  four-phase  plan  of  action  is  detailed,  providing 
milestone criteria, numerical convergence tolerances, and journal submission targets for high-impact Q1  
publications.

1. Introduction and Objectives
The primary objective of the Surfactant-Dynamics-Sim initiative is to establish the first open-
source, multiscale simulation framework coupling molecular-level surfactant thermodynamic properties  
with continuum-scale dynamic transport phenomena [1,2]. While standard molecular modeling toolkits  
and  computational  fluid  dynamics  packages  operate  in  mutual  isolation,  our  integrated  framework 
bridges this gap by marrying the molecular parameter calculation engine of SurfactantKit with the stiff  
differential-algebraic equation (DAE) solvers of Scilab-Xcos [3-5].
To ensure seamless execution, this document serves as the master operational plan. It provides  
complete specifications for data acquisition, hardware utilization across local and cloud tiers, AI agent  
task allocations, and a phase-by-phase implementation schedule designed to yield premium scientific  
publications in premier journals such as Journal of Colloid and Interface Science (JCIS), Chemical  
Engineering Science (CES), and Colloids and Surfaces A [6-8].
2. Experimental Data Mining Protocols and Target Datasets
To  rigorously  validate  the  computational  models  across  all  dynamic  regimes,  experimental 
datasets must be extracted, digitized, and curated from peer-reviewed literature into standardized JSON  
and CSV formats [9]. Four core experimental categories are required:
2.1. Dynamic Surface Tensiometry (DST) Datasets
Dynamic surface tension curves (gamma(t) versus time) spanning short adsorption times (1  
millisecond) to long-term equilibrium (10 3 seconds) are required for baseline model calibration [10,11]. 
Target  surfactant  systems  must  encompass  diverse  molecular  architectures:  (1)  anionic  surfactants, 
specifically  sodium  dodecyl  sulfate  (SDS)  and  sodium  bis(2-ethylhexyl)  sulfosuccinate  (AOT);  (2) 

3
cationic  surfactants,  including  cetyltrimethylammonium  bromide  (CTAB)  and  
dodecyltrimethylammonium bromide (DTAB); (3) nonionic ethoxylated alcohols, such as Triton X-100  
and  C12E8;  and  (4)  representative  cationic  gemini  surfactants  (alkanediyl-alpha,omega-
bis(dimethylalkylammonium bromide), 12-s-12 series) [12,13]. For each system, digitized records must  
capture  temperature,  bulk  concentration  (from  0.01*CMC  to  10*CMC),  maximum  surface  excess 
(Gamma_inf), molecular headgroup area (a0), and diffusion coefficient (D).
2.2. Large-Amplitude Oscillatory Dilatational (LAOD) Rheology Datasets
Interfacial dilatational rheometry records obtained via pulsating bubble or oscillating pendant  
drop  tensiometers  (e.g.,  Kruss  PAT-1M,  Teclis  Tracker)  provide  complex  viscoelastic  moduli  as 
functions of angular frequency (omega = 0.05 to 10 rad/s) and strain amplitude (epsilon = Delta A / A0  
= 0.01 to 0.40)  [14,15]. Mined data must include the storage dilatational modulus (E'), loss modulus  
(E''), loss angle (theta), and higher harmonic intensity ratios (I_2omega / I_omega, I_3omega / I_omega)  
to benchmark non-linear viscoelastic distortions [16].
2.3. Microfluidic Droplet Breakup and Pinch-Off Timeseries
High-speed microphotographic datasets of droplet generation in planar flow-focusing and T-
junction  microchannels  provide  critical  validation  for  dynamic  interfacial  area  generation  [17,18]. 
Extracted  parameters  must  comprise  channel  orifice  dimensions  (width  w  =  20-100  micrometers), 
continuous and dispersed flow rates (Q_c, Q_d), dynamic capillary numbers (Ca = mu * v / gamma(t)),  
neck thinning trajectories (h_neck(t) versus time), and resulting droplet polydispersity indices.
2.4. Thin Liquid Film and Dynamic Foam Lamella Drainage
Thin  film  balance  (TFB)  interferometric  measurements  of  microscopic  foam  lamellae  
(Scheludko cell data) are required to validate drainage hydrodynamics [19,20]. Target datasets comprise 

4
film  thickness  decay  curves  (h(t)  versus  time),  equilibrium  film  thickness  (h_eq),  critical  rupture 
thickness (h_crit), and disjoining pressure isotherms (Pi(h)) across ionic strengths from 10-4 to 10-1 M.

3. Hardware Specifications and Computational Allocation
The computational architecture combines local edge processing with high-performance cloud  
accelerators to optimize turnaround time and numerical efficiency.
3.1. Local Host Environment: Windows 11 Laptop
The host workstation is equipped with an Intel Core Ultra 7 256V processor (8 high-IPC physical 
cores) and 16 GB on-package LPDDR5X memory. This environment hosts Scilab 2025.0.0 and Xcos,  
utilizing the Intel Math Kernel Library (MKL) for accelerated matrix decompositions and SUNDIALS  
CVode/IDA numerical integration  [21]. All local Scilab scripts, ODE state-space solvers, and Xcos  
graphical block diagrams (.zcos) execute directly within this tier.
3.2. Intel Arc 140V GPU (8 GB) Utilization
The  integrated  Intel  Arc  140V  GPU,  based  on  the  advanced  Xe2  microarchitecture  with 
dedicated XMX AI engines, is allocated specifically for local machine learning acceleration [22]. While 
Scilab itself relies on CPU-bound MKL vectorization, the Intel Arc GPU is harnessed via PyTorch with  

5
DirectML (torch-directml) or Intel Extension for PyTorch (IPEX) to train and execute Physics-Informed  
Neural Networks (PINNs) and DeepONet neural operators  [23]. These neural surrogates approximate  
the non-local Ward-Tordai convolutional integral in sub-millisecond inference times, providing instant  
property evaluations to the Scilab solver.
3.3. Remote Cloud GPU Environment: Kaggle Compute
For tasks exceeding local workstation memory or requiring prolonged multi-GPU parallelism, a  
dedicated Kaggle cloud account (equipped with dual Nvidia Tesla T4 or P100/A100 accelerators) is  
utilized [24]. This environment is deployed for: (1) coarse-grained dissipative particle dynamics (DPD)  
or molecular dynamics (GROMACS) to  compute  structural  micellar  aggregation  numbers; and (2)  
extensive Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) Bayesian parameter estimation sweeps across thousands  
of  experimental  DST  curves.  Job  submissions,  monitoring,  and  artifact  downloads  are  managed 
programmatically via the Kaggle API.
3.4. Optional Linux Environment: Windows Subsystem for Linux (WSL2)
For headless Linux compilation, Modelica C-code generation, and automated regression testing  
pipelines, Windows Subsystem for Linux (WSL2 Ubuntu 24.04 LTS) is configured on the host machine. 
This tier ensures platform-independent reproducibility.
4. Agentic Collaboration and Division of Labor
To achieve maximum productivity, project workflows are partitioned between two specialized  
artificial intelligence agents: Antigravity (agy), operating locally on the host machine, and Arena AI,  
operating within a high-capability browser sandbox [25].
4.1. Role and Responsibilities of Antigravity (Local Execution Agent)
Antigravity functions as the core numerical and systems engineering agent. Its responsibilities  
comprise:  (1)  developing  and  maintaining  Scilab  scripts,  Xcos  block  diagrams,  and  Python-Scilab 

6
bridging  routines;  (2)  compiling  and  running  local  simulations  via  the  native  Scilab  engine;  (3) 
managing the Intel Arc GPU for surrogate model training and running Kaggle GPU cloud jobs; (4)  
maintaining  the  GitHub  repository  (prashant-kotian/surfactant-dynamics-sim);  and  (5)  authoring 
publication manuscripts strictly compliant with MANUSCRIPT_FORMATTING_SPEC.md.
4.2. Role and Responsibilities of Arena AI (Browser and Data Mining Agent)
Arena  AI  functions  as  the  research  intelligence  and  literature  acquisition  agent.  Its  
responsibilities comprise: (1) querying academic databases (ScienceDirect, ACS, RSC, Springer) to  
locate target experimental studies; (2) extracting and digitizing graphical data curves from scanned  
PDFs using automated image processing and WebPlotDigitizer routines; (3) packaging digitized records  
into curated JSON schema files; and (4) uploading raw papers, literature syntheses, and digitized data  
tables to the shared Google Drive repository (kotianinterface@gmail.com) and creating pull requests on  
GitHub.
4.3. Synchronization Infrastructure
Bidirectional  communication  between  Antigravity  and  Arena  AI  is  established  across  two 
channels:  (1)  GitHub  (prashant-kotian/surfactant-dynamics-sim)  serves  as  the  primary  version-
controlled  codebase  for  models,  scripts,  and  structured  JSON  datasets;  and  (2)  Google  Drive 
(kotianinterface@gmail.com)  serves as the  storage  repository  for  large  binary files,  high-resolution 
microscope videos, raw literature PDFs, and intermediate simulation state files.

5. Detailed Four-Phase Plan of Action
The project is structured into four sequential, milestone-driven phases spanning an eight-month  
development lifecycle.
5.1. Phase 1: Analytical Verification and Baseline Solvers (Months 1-2)
Objective:  Establish  the  foundational  Scilab-Xcos  numerical  architecture  and  verify  solver 
precision against exact analytical solutions.
Key Deliverables: (1) Construct Xcos state-space block diagrams for diffusion-controlled planar  
adsorption;  (2)  Benchmark  SUNDIALS  CVode  solver  against  asymptotic  short-time  (gamma(t) 
proportional to t 0.5) and long-time (gamma(t) proportional to t -0.5) Ward-Tordai solutions; (3) Verify  
linear Lucassen-van den Tempel (LVDT) viscoelastic limits for small strain amplitudes (epsilon < 0.02);  
and (4) Initialize the automated continuous integration pipeline on GitHub.
5.2. Phase 2: Experimental Data Mining and Model Validation (Months 3-4)
Objective: Ingest digitized experimental datasets from Arena AI and calibrate dynamic kinetic  
parameters across model surfactant classes.
Key  Deliverables:  (1)  Arena  AI  curates  50+  benchmark  DST  and  LAOD  datasets  into 
data/curated/; (2) Antigravity executes non-linear least-squares fitting in Scilab to extract kinetic rate  
constants (ka, kd) and micellar relaxation times (tau1, tau2); (3) Validate dynamic surface tension  
predictions against Kruss PAT-1M measurements for SDS, CTAB, and Triton X-100; and (4) Deploy  
Intel Arc GPU surrogate models for real-time Ward-Tordai convolution.
5.3. Phase 3: Non-Equilibrium Discovery and Parameter Sweeps (Months 5-6)

8
Objective:  Explore  unmapped  physical  regimes,  identify  novel  non-linear  phenomena,  and 
construct predictive engineering regime maps.
Key Deliverables: (1) Map higher-order Fourier harmonic distortion (2*omega, 3*omega) under  
large-amplitude area oscillations (epsilon = 0.20-0.50); (2) Simulate dynamic Marangoni tip-streaming  
in microfluidic junctions, identifying critical Capillary numbers (Ca_crit) governing satellite droplet  
suppression; (3) Model acoustic resonance in thin liquid film drainage, predicting subharmonic rupture  
frequencies; and (4) Execute multi-GPU Bayesian sensitivity analyses on Kaggle.
5.4. Phase 4: Manuscript Preparation and Journal Submission (Months 7-8)
Objective: Package theoretical formulations, simulation codes, and experimental validations into  
peer-reviewed manuscripts.
Key  Deliverables:  (1)  Author  Manuscript  1  on  Non-linear  LAOD  Rheology  for  Journal  of 
Colloid and Interface Science (JCIS); (2) Author Manuscript 2 on Microfluidic Marangoni Droplet  
Breakup for Chemical Engineering Science (CES); (3) Publish open-source software release on GitHub  
with  full  documentation  and  interactive  examples;  and  (4)  Verify  all  manuscripts  against  
MANUSCRIPT_FORMATTING_SPEC.md.
6. Conclusion and Immediate Next Steps
This  operational  roadmap  provides  an  unambiguous,  rigorous  framework  for  transforming 
SurfactantKit and Scilab-Xcos into an internationally recognized computational platform for dynamic  
colloid science. By combining local Intel hardware and cloud GPU acceleration with a disciplined  
division of labor between Antigravity and Arena AI, the project is uniquely positioned to produce  
reproducible, high-impact scientific discoveries.

Abstract
A central challenge in contemporary colloid and interface science is the profound computational  
divide between molecular-scale equilibrium thermodynamics and continuum-scale process dynamics.  
While existing computational toolkits excel at calculating equilibrium properties such as critical micelle  
concentration, packing parameters, and static adsorption isotherms, they fail to capture the transient,  
non-equilibrium phenomena that govern real-world applications including microfluidic emulsification,  
dynamic foam drainage, enhanced oil recovery, and pulmonary surfactant mechanics. In this work, we  
propose a synergistic integration between SurfactantKit, an open-source Python library for molecular  
and thermodynamic interfacial properties, and Scilab-Xcos, a high-performance open-source numerical  
environment and dynamic block-diagram simulator. By coupling SurfactantKit's microscopic molecular  
descriptors and adsorption equations of state with the stiff differential-algebraic equation solvers of  
Scilab-Xcos, researchers can simulate dynamic Marangoni stresses, micellar relaxation kinetics, and  
transient interfacial dilation across millisecond-to-minute timescales. We present four specific, high-
impact research proposals designed for publication in premier peer-reviewed journals (such as Journal of 
Colloid and Interface Science, Langmuir, Chemical Engineering Science, and Computers & Chemical  
Engineering): (1) nonlinear large-amplitude oscillatory dilatational rheology and harmonic distortion;  
(2) dynamic Marangoni tip-streaming and droplet breakup in microfluidic junctions; (3) acoustic and  
mechanical  resonance  in  thin  liquid  film  foam  drainage;  and  (4)  an  open-source,  reproducible 

2
benchmark for dynamic colloid engineering. Furthermore, an executable mathematical formulation and  
Scilab prototype are provided, establishing a clear operational roadmap for generating novel, publishable 
scientific breakthroughs without reliance on proprietary simulation suites.
Keywords: SurfactantKit, Scilab, Xcos, dynamic surface tension, interfacial rheology, Marangoni stress,

1. Introduction
Surfactant formulations represent the cornerstone of modern chemical manufacturing, personal  
care formulations, pharmaceutical drug delivery systems, enhanced oil recovery (EOR), and mineral  
flotation  [1,2]. Over several decades, academic and industrial research has relied predominantly on  
equilibrium characterization techniques, such as equilibrium surface tension isotherms, critical micelle  
concentration  (CMC)  measurements,  and  static  contact  angle  measurements  [3-5].  In  parallel, 
computational  colloid  chemistry  has  bifurcated  into  two  disparate  extremes:  atomistic  molecular 
dynamics (MD) simulations on the one hand, and classical computational fluid dynamics (CFD) on the  
other [6,7].
Atomistic and coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations provide invaluable microscopic  
insights into micellar aggregation numbers, headgroup hydration shells, and bilayer self-assembly [8,9]. 
However,  MD  is  fundamentally  constrained  by  spatial  and  temporal  boundaries,  rarely  capturing 
phenomena  beyond 10-100 nanoseconds and tens of nanometers, which renders it computationally  
intractable for macroscopic transport processes. Conversely, conventional CFD and process engineering  
simulators  treat  interfacial  tension  as  an  instantaneous,  scalar  constant  or  rely  on  crude  empirical 
correlations that ignore molecular relaxation phenomena  [10,11]. Real-world interfacial operations—
such  as  droplet  breakup  in  high-shear  microfluidic  mixers,  rapid  bubble  expansion  during  lung 

3
respiration, and transient foam film rupture—occur in non-equilibrium regimes spanning milliseconds to  
tens of seconds [12-14].
To bridge this mesoscale continuum divide, an open-source dynamic co-simulation architecture  
is  required.  Recently,  the  open-source  package  SurfactantKit  has  emerged  to  compute  molecular 
descriptors,  critical  packing  parameters,  mixed-micelle  non-ideality,  and  Ward-Tordai  adsorption 
kinetics directly from chemical structure  [15]. Simultaneously, Scilab and its dynamic block-diagram  
environment Xcos provide industrial-grade, open-source differential-algebraic equation (DAE) solvers,  
event-driven hybrid simulation engines, and Modelica-based physical modeling capabilities [16]. In this 
paper,  we  systematically  evaluate  whether  coupling  Scilab-Xcos  with  SurfactantKit  can  generate 
genuinely novel scientific findings capable of supporting premium Q1 publications, outline four high-
impact investigation proposals, and provide a concrete implementation blueprint.
2. Computational Architecture and Bridging Framework
The proposed computational synergy couples molecular-level thermodynamics with dynamic  
continuum state-space integration.  Figure 1  illustrates the bidirectional communication between the  
microscopic property engine (SurfactantKit) and the macroscopic dynamic simulator (Scilab-Xcos).
2.1. Thermodynamic Parameter Engine: SurfactantKit
SurfactantKit is structured as a modular Python framework designed for theoretical and applied  
surfactant science. Its core modules evaluate molecular structure from simplified molecular-input line-
entry system (SMILES) strings, determining tail volume (v), effective extended chain length (lc), and  
polar  headgroup  area  (a0)  to  predict  the  critical  packing  parameter  (CPP  =  v  /  (a0  *  lc))  [17]. 
Furthermore, the library incorporates established thermodynamic formulations, including the Langmuir-
Szyszkowski, Frumkin, and Temkin adsorption isotherms, Rubingh-Rosen regular solution theory for  

4
multicomponent  mixed  micelles,  hydrophilic-lipophilic  difference  (HLD-NAC)  phase  behavior 
coordinates, and the Ward-Tordai diffusion-controlled dynamic adsorption integral [18-20].
2.2. Dynamic State-Space Integrator: Scilab and Xcos
Scilab provides a matrix-based computational environment equipped with the SUNDIALS solver 
suite, specifically CVode for stiff ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and IDA for differential-
algebraic equations (DAEs) [21]. Xcos extends Scilab with a graphical, multi-domain dynamic modeling 
canvas analogous to Simulink. Within Xcos, physical processes are represented as interconnected blocks  
that support continuous integration, discrete-time sampling, zero-crossing event detection, and Modelica  
physical components [22]. This makes Xcos exceptionally well-suited for solving boundary-layer mass  
transfer equations, transient interfacial area deformations, dynamic Marangoni stress balances, and thin-
film drainage hydrodynamics.
2.3. Bidirectional Coupling Protocols and Data Flow
To achieve seamless co-simulation between Python-based SurfactantKit and Scilab-Xcos, three  
integration architectures are established: (1) an in-process direct C-API or Python gateway, where  
Scilab's built-in Python integration executes SurfactantKit routines directly during runtime; (2) a high-
speed inter-process communication (IPC) protocol using shared memory or local loopback sockets  
exchanging JSON-serialized state vectors; and (3) a Functional Mock-up Interface (FMI) standard where 
SurfactantKit models are compiled into Functional Mock-up Units (FMUs) ingested by Xcos [23]. Table 
1 contrasts this open-source architecture with conventional proprietary alternatives.

3. Four Breakthrough Research Proposals for Premium Publications
By merging SurfactantKit's molecular thermodynamics with Xcos's continuum dynamic solvers,  
researchers can tackle complex interfacial problems that currently remain unsolved due to software  
bifurcation. Below, four concrete research directions are detailed.
3.1.  Nonlinear  Large-Amplitude  Oscillatory  Dilatational  (LAOD)  Rheology  and  Harmonic 
Marangoni Stress
In interfacial dilatational rheometry, sinusoidal area oscillations are imposed on a pendant drop  
or bubble (A(t) = A0 * [1 + epsilon * sin(omega * t)]). Current literature predominantly evaluates the  
dynamic interfacial tension through linear viscoelastic theory, as formulated by Lucassen and van den  
Tempel (LVDT), which is strictly valid only in the limit of infinitesimal strain amplitudes (epsilon <  
0.05)  [24,25].  However,  industrial  atomization,  high-speed  coating,  and  physiological  alveolar 
expansion involve large cyclic deformations (epsilon = 0.20-0.50) where linear assumptions collapse.
Under large-amplitude oscillations, nonlinear surface coverage, cohesive Frumkin intermolecular 
lateral interactions (parameter beta), and finite micellar breakdown kinetics (Aniansson-Wall relaxation  
times tau1 and tau2) generate asymmetric Marangoni stress profiles [26,27]. By coupling SurfactantKit's 
non-ideal equation of state with Xcos's nonlinear DAE solver, one can model the exact boundary-layer  
diffusion flux coupled with micellar source/sink terms (dM/dt = -(M - Meq) / tau2). The resulting  
dynamic  surface  tension  response  exhibits  higher-order  Fourier  harmonics  (2*omega,  3*omega). 
Discovering  how  specific  surfactant  molecular  structures  (e.g.,  gemini  surfactants  vs.  single-chain 
ethoxylates) generate distinct harmonic fingerprints provides a novel scientific breakthrough directly  
suitable for Journal of Colloid and Interface Science (JCIS) or Langmuir.

3.2. Dynamic Marangoni Tip-Streaming and Droplet Breakup in Microfluidic Cross-Flow Junctions
Microfluidic droplet generation in flow-focusing geometries operates at millisecond capillary  
timescales. When an aqueous surfactant solution is sheared by an immiscible continuous oil phase, fresh  
interface is rapidly generated at the droplet tip (dA/dt >> 0) [28,29]. Because surfactant diffusion and  
adsorption require finite time, dynamic surface tension (gamma(t)) at the protruding neck is substantially 
higher  than  the  equilibrium  surface  tension  (gamma_eq).  This  gradient  creates  intense  Marangoni 
stresses  (tau_M  =  grad(gamma))  that  act  opposite  to  the  fluid  drag,  either  delaying  pinch-off  or 
triggering tip-streaming of sub-micron satellite droplets [30].
Using Xcos, researchers can construct a lumped-parameter hydrodynamic network simulating  
the coupled continuous-phase pressure drop, neck contraction velocity, and time-dependent capillary  
number (Ca(t) = mu * v / gamma(t)), with gamma(t) computed in real-time by SurfactantKit's numerical  
Ward-Tordai kernel. This enables the discovery of a universal regime map predicting the transition from  
stable dripping to irregular jetting as a function of the surfactant's characteristic adsorption Damkohler  
number  (Da  =  t_hydro  /  t_ads).  This  investigation  directly  addresses  the  core  scope  of  Chemical 
Engineering Science or Soft Matter.
3.3. Acoustic and Mechanical Resonance in Dynamic Foam Lamella Drainage and Rupture
A critical problem in foam science is understanding why specific mixed surfactant formulations  
(e.g., anionic/zwitterionic or catanionic pairs) resist mechanical vibration and acoustic noise during  
industrial transport or firefighting operations  [31,32]. Classical foam drainage models rely on steady-
state  Plateau  border  drainage  equations,  neglecting  the  oscillatory  perturbation  of  the  liquid  film 
thickness [33].
SurfactantKit  calculates  the  disjoining  pressure  isotherm  Pi(h),  incorporating  electrostatic 
double-layer repulsion via the nonlinear Poisson-Boltzmann equation, van der Waals attraction, and  
short-range steric hydration, alongside the Gibbs surface elasticity (EG = -d(gamma) / d(ln(Gamma)))  

7
[34]. In Xcos, the thin-film Reynolds drainage equation (dh/dt) is integrated under external sinusoidal  
pressure  fluctuations.  Coupling  the  dynamic  surface  tension  recovery  to  film  drainage  reveals 
subharmonic resonance modes: at critical vibrational frequencies, localized Marangoni backflow fails to  
heal the thinned region, triggering catastrophic film rupture at a critical thickness (hcrit). Publishing  
these frequency-dependent foam rupture thresholds offers a high-impact contribution for Colloids and  
Surfaces A: Physicochemical and Engineering Aspects or JCIS.

4. Concrete Scilab-Xcos Simulation Prototype
To demonstrate operational feasibility, we present an executable mathematical formulation and  
Scilab simulation script coupling dynamic interfacial dilation with surfactant diffusion and adsorption  
kinetics. The governing equations consider an expanding interface with surface excess Gamma(t), bulk  
subsurface concentration C(0, t), and bulk concentration C0.
The dynamic conservation of surfactant mass at the interface is described by:
d(Gamma)/dt = ja - Gamma * d(ln(A))/dt
where ja is the net adsorption flux given by the kinetic Langmuir-Szyszkowski model, ja = ka *  
C(0, t) * (Gamma_inf - Gamma) - kd * Gamma, and d(ln(A))/dt represents the dilatational rate imposed  
by cyclic mechanical expansion (A(t) = A0 * [1 + epsilon * sin(omega * t)]). Under diffusion-limited  
conditions, the subsurface concentration C(0, t) satisfies the Ward-Tordai convolutional relationship, or  
in a state-space approximation, a two-compartment boundary layer model with characteristic diffusion  
boundary thickness delta = (pi * D / omega)^0.5 [36].
The dynamic surface tension gamma(t) is coupled via the Szyszkowski equation of state:
gamma(t) = gamma_0 - n * R * T * Gamma_inf * ln(1 + K_L * C(0, t))
The corresponding Scilab implementation script is displayed below in Code Block 1.
Code Block 1. Scilab implementation of dynamic surface tension under large-amplitude oscillatory area dilation.
// Scilab Prototype: Dynamic Surface Tension Under Oscillatory Area Dilation
clear; clc;
// Parameters loaded from SurfactantKit
gamma0   = 72.8;     // Pure solvent surface tension (mN/m)
Gamma_inf= 3.8e-6;   // Maximum surface excess (mol/m^2)
n_ion    = 1.0;      // Nonionic / ionic factor
R_gas    = 8.314;    // Universal gas constant (J/mol/K)
Temp     = 298.15;   // Temperature (K)
ka       = 1.5e-1;   // Adsorption rate constant (m^3/mol/s)
kd       = 1.2e-3;   // Desorption rate constant (1/s)
C0       = 5.0e-3;   // Bulk concentration (mol/m^3)
D_diff   = 4.5e-10;  // Molecular diffusion coefficient (m^2/s)

9
// Oscillatory deformation parameters
omega    = 0.5;      // Frequency (rad/s)
eps      = 0.20;     // Large strain amplitude (20%)
// Define state-space derivative function
function dxdt = surfactant_dynamics(t, x)
    Gamma_t = x(1);
    C_sub   = x(2);
    // Dynamic area dilation rate: d(ln A)/dt
    A_t      = 1.0 + eps * sin(omega * t);
    dlnA_dt  = (eps * omega * cos(omega * t)) / A_t;
    // Kinetic adsorption flux
    j_ads    = ka * C_sub * (Gamma_inf - Gamma_t) - kd * Gamma_t;
    // Subsurface boundary-layer mass transfer
    delta    = sqrt(%pi * D_diff / omega);
    j_diff   = (2.0 * D_diff / delta) * (C0 - C_sub);
    // State derivatives: [dGamma/dt; dC_sub/dt]
    dGamma   = j_ads - Gamma_t * dlnA_dt;
    dC_sub   = (j_diff - j_ads) / delta;
    dxdt     = [dGamma; dC_sub];
endfunction
// Time integration grid (0 to 60 seconds)
t = linspace(0, 60, 1200);
x0 = [0.8 * Gamma_inf; C0]; // Initial state
// Execute ODE solver via CVode algorithm
sol = ode(x0, 0, t, surfactant_dynamics);
// Calculate dynamic tension time series
Gamma_res = sol(1, :);
RT_factor = n_ion * R_gas * Temp * Gamma_inf * 1e3; // mN/m
gamma_t   = gamma0 + RT_factor * log(1.0 - (Gamma_res / Gamma_inf));
printf('Simulation completed successfully. Points integrated: %d\n', length(t));

To translate these theoretical models into published papers in premier Q1 journals, a disciplined  
four-phase execution roadmap is required:
Phase  1:  Verification  Against  Standard  Analytical  Solutions  (Months  1-2).  Benchmark  the 
Scilab-Xcos integrator against pure diffusion-controlled Ward-Tordai solutions for planar interfaces in  
quiescent  solutions,  confirming  zero  numerical  drift  and  convergence  of  the  SUNDIALS  CVode 
algorithm [37].
Phase 2: Validation Using Open Experimental Datasets (Months 3-4). Cross-validate simulated

validated  figures,  and  open-source  GitHub  repositories  according  to  journal-specific  formatting 
requirements, prioritizing Journal of Colloid and Interface Science and Chemical Engineering Science.

The  integration  of  Scilab-Xcos  with  SurfactantKit  resolves  a  longstanding  methodological 
bottleneck  in  interfacial  engineering.  By  marrying  the  microscopic  molecular  descriptors  and 
thermodynamic rigor of SurfactantKit with the powerful, open-source dynamic continuum solvers of  
Scilab-Xcos, researchers can explore non-equilibrium interfacial physics that were previously either  
computationally inaccessible or restricted to costly proprietary software. The four proposed research  
avenues—spanning nonlinear LAOD rheology, microfluidic tip-streaming, acoustic foam resonance, and

ABSTRACT
Large amplitude oscillatory dilatational (LAOD) surface rheology is an emerging tool for characterising the 
nonlinear mechanical behaviour of air-liquid and liquid-liquid interfaces in multiphase systems, such as in foams 
and emulsions. These interfaces, stabilised by surfactants or emulsifiers, exhibit complex viscoelastic properties 
that significantly influence bubble and droplet stability under large deformation conditions. While conventional 
methods mainly focus on linear viscoelastic behaviour, LAOD allows us to explore the nonlinear deformation 
behaviour of interfaces, offering deeper insights into the mechanical properties of interfaces upon large 
deformation.
This review highlights key advances in the dilatational rheology of interfacial films, providing a compre-
hensive background on LAOD experimental design, data collection, and analysis techniques. Recent de-
velopments in data analysis have revolutionised the LAOD technique. While Lissajous plots gave qualitative 
insights in the past, the new general stress decomposition (GSD) method allows for more quantitative analysis of 
the nonlinearities. GSD can quantitatively separate density-driven and actual rheological (i.e. network) contri-
butions in the stress response. This separation reveals previously hidden rheological responses, allowing more 
accurate quantification of interfacial mechanics and deeper mechanistic insights into interfacial stabilisation 
phenomena. We will discuss how these LAOD methodologies have significantly enhanced our understanding of 
interfaces stabilised by surfactants, proteins, particles, and mixed systems.
The main take-home-message: The emergence of advanced LAOD methodologies has redefined how we 
characterise nonlinear interfacial mechanics. Future research should focus on expanding GSD applications and 
revisiting previously studied interfaces with GSD, which will quantify the previously qualitatively described 
behaviour of interfacial films.
1. Introduction
Multiphase systems are ubiquitous in life, and examples are emul-
sions and foams in pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and foods [1,2,3,4]. 
Multiphase systems also play a key role in the function of the human 
body, for instance, where cell bilayers play a key role in cell 
functionality [5]. As a result, there is widespread research focus on these 
multiphase systems, with a specific emphasis on the boundary between 
the immiscible phases: the interface.
The stabilisation of liquid-liquid or gas-liquid interfaces is key in 
many applications. To successfully stabilise these thermodynamically 
unstable systems, stabilising molecules are needed, such as emulsifiers

interfacial properties and will, therefore, determine to a great extent the 
stability of emulsions and foams during deformations that may occur 
during emulsion or foam processing [6]. Depending on the type of sta-
biliser, the interface can form interfacial layers with different types of 
rheological/mechanical properties, and examples are elastic or visco-
elastic layers. The interfacial mechanics play a major role in the stability 
of droplets or bubbles during deformations. Other external factors such 
as the system’s pH, ionic strength or the presence of other components 
might further influence the interfacial properties [7,8,9,6] Under-
standing how a certain emulsifier behaves at the interface, taking 
external factors into account is thus crucial for understanding and pre-
dicting the process, temperature and storage stability of any multiphase 
system as well as to predict the behaviour of an emulsifier in stabilising 
an interface.
The behaviour of these interfacial stabilisers at the interface can be 
studied using rheology to obtain insights into the mechanical properties 
of an interface [10,11,12], imaging/microscopy techniques to obtain 
visual insights into the structure [13,14] and computationally to gain 
insights into molecular interactions and arrangements [15]. In this re-
view, we will mainly focus on the mechanical properties, which are 
studied by interfacial (or surface) rheology.
In interfacial/surface rheology, an interface is deformed in a 
controlled manner while carefully analysing the stress response. Two 
main modes are commonly used: surface shear rheology and surface 
dilatational rheology [16]. In both, the deformations are commonly 
applied in oscillatory mode, where the deformation amplitude and fre-
quency of oscillations can be varied. The deformation conditions can be 
used to test the mechanical response in two regimes: the linear visco-
elastic (LVE) or the nonlinear viscoelastic (NLVE) regime. The LVE 
regime is the region where the interfacial microstructure recovers at the 
same rate as the disruption induced by the deformation. The NLVE 
regime is the regime where the disruption is more pronounced than the 
recovery, resulting in a breakdown of the microstructure. These regimes 
can be found by performing amplitude (or strain) sweeps, where the 
deformation amplitude is gradually increased. By measuring the first 
harmonic-based storage and loss moduli, one would find moduli inde-
pendent of amplitude in the LVE regime (often <5 % deformation 
amplitude), and typically a decrease (and occasionally an increase) of 
moduli for the NLVE regime [17,18,19]. The rheological deformations 
in the NLVE regime are considered large deformations, often called large 
amplitude oscillatory dilatation (LAOD) or shear (LAOS).
During these large deformations, nonlinearities in the stress output 
emerge, due to the breakdown of the interfacial microstructure [16]. 
These nonlinearities often complicate the accurate analysis of interfacial 
film behaviour, especially when using conventional analysis methods, 
such as calculating the first harmonic-based moduli, which are often 
incorporated in commercially available rheological instruments. This 
inaccurate analysis is unfortunate, as deformation in the NLVE regime is 
often necessary to assess the stability of a system exposed to large de-
formations that occur during processing in industrial settings, such as 
the stability of air bubbles and oil droplets under high shear. Non-
linearities in the stress response can be accurately analysed in surface 
shear rheology, using methods previously established for nonlinear bulk 
shear rheology [20]. A well-known example is using Lissajous-(Bow-
ditch) curves to qualitatively analyse the raw stress response. These 
curves can be further quantitatively characterised by determining shear 
stiffening/thickening factors and dissipated energy values [21]. In 
addition, methods have been derived to also include the higher har-
monics, thus the nonlinearities, into so-called multi-harmonic moduli 
[22]. While in (surface) shear rheology, nonlinear analysis has received 
considerable attention, this is not the case for interfacial dilatational 
rheology.
In the past years, LAOD has seen increasing use, but it is still mostly 
studied in a qualitative fashion using Lissajous plots. This analysis 
already gives more insights into the mechanical properties of an 
interfacial layer, compared to solely studying the first-harmonic-based 
surface dilatational moduli. This technique combined with microstruc-
ture (imaging) analysis, using, for instance, ellipsometry, synchrotron 
scattering techniques, and electron or atomic force microscopy on 
deposited films, has yielded new insights into interface stabilisation 
([23,18,24,14]). In addition, a new method has recently been developed 
to quantitatively analyse the nonlinearities of dilatational rheological 
output, which could help in further evolving the field of LAOD [25].
In this review, we aim to summarise these recent advances in LAOD 
rheology analysis and quantification and provide key insights into the 
dilatational rheological properties of interfacial films. We will first give 
a background on dilatational rheology, including analysis techniques, 
experimental design, and data analysis, to describe the state-of-the-art 
before diving into the recent developments. The latter will be exten-
sively discussed to guide readers through the key elements of LAOD 
analysis in both qualitative and quantitative ways. Finally, we show how 
to relate the rheological findings to the microstructure of various types 
of interface stabilisers, such as small surfactants and large biopolymers 
(e.g., proteins and polysaccharides).
2. Introduction to large amplitude oscillatory dilatation (LAOD)
2.1. Dilatational surface rheology
Many destabilisation mechanisms in multiphase systems involve 
substantial changes in interfacial area. This is where dilatational inter-
facial rheology comes into play, which studies the mechanical properties 
of interfaces, such as liquid-liquid (e.g., oil-water) or gas-liquid 
(generally air-water) interfaces, under conditions where the total area 
of the interface is changed [26]. It focuses on the properties of the in-
terfaces upon compression and expansion, when an all-sided in-plane 
compression or extension is performed. The surface mass is, therefore, 
not always conserved during these types of deformation [27]. Upon 
compression, the interfacial density of surface-active agents may in-
crease, which can cause the desorption of some surfactants from the 
interface back into the bulk. Whether the molecules desorb back to the 
bulk or remain at the interface will be determined by the desorption 
energy. Analogously, upon extension, the interfacial density decreases, 
often facilitating the absorption of new molecules from the bulk to the 
newly created interfacial area.
Different instruments can be used to perform interfacial dilatational 
experiments [28]. The Langmuir-Blodget trough (commonly referred to 
as Langmuir trough) was the first instrument to be employed, based on 
the early experiments of Agnes Pockels on thin films of amphiphilic 
substances at the surface of water [29]. Irvin Langmuir and Katherine 
Blodgett then improved the equipment, which consists of a liquid 
reservoir, with movable barriers at the interface, that allow for the 
compression and expansion of the interface [30]. The dynamic surface 
tension (γd) during the deformations is measured using a Wilhelmy 
plate, connected to a balance [31]. However, the Langmuir trough does 
not provide a purely dilatational response, but a combination of shear 
and dilatation, due to the two barriers compressing the interface from 
just two sides, essentially performing a uniaxial compression rather than 
an all-sided one [32]. A fully all-sided deformation can be achieved by 
an equal deformation from all directions, which can, for instance, be 
achieved by using a recently invented quadrotrough [33].
A different method to measure surface tension was described in two 
early works by [34,35], based on the so-called “oil-drop” method, where 
the surface tension was for the first time calculated in real-time to 
measure lipase kinetics from the contour of a rising oil droplet in water, 
also known as an inverted pendant drop. Based on this rising drop setup, 
a dynamic drop tensiometer (DT) was later developed [36], where a 
droplet of a few millimetres in diameter at the tip of a needle could be 
deformed by changing the volume, using a syringe, thereby expanding 
or contracting the interface. These deformations are, in general, a better 
approximation for an all-sided extension/compression, than those

2.2. Experimental design: Dilatational rheology using drop tensiometry
When working with a drop tensiometer, the measurement can be 
carried out using either the rising/emerging drop/bubble/inverted 
pendant drop method or the pendant/hanging drop method [26]. In the 
rising method, a droplet of the lower density phase or an air bubble is 
created at the tip of a curved needle, immersed in a cuvette filled with 
the higher density phase (often water). The phases are reversed when 
using the pendant drop method, where a droplet of the high-density 
phase is created at the tip of a straight needle, which is surrounded by 
the lower-density phase (which can be oil or air). The choice of method 
depends on several factors, such as 1) the available amount of emulsifier 
or oil phase, where the phase with a lower availability is typically put in 
the droplet, and 2) transparency (the continuous phase should be suf-
ficiently transparent to capture the droplet contour).
The exact calibration of a drop tensiometer is vital for obtaining 
accurate measurements (Fig. 1). For the correct calculation of the 
interfacial tension, we require knowledge of the horizontal (cr) and 
vertical (cz) calibration factors for the conversion of the CCD-camera 
pixels to SI units (millimetres) [39]. These parameters are determined 
during the optical calibration. The optical calibration of the instrument 
can be done in three different ways: calibration with a sphere of known 
radius, calibration with a capillary of known radius, or calibration with a 
pure liquid or water at a specific temperature. As reported by [39], the 
accuracy of such conversion factors should be better than 0.1 %.
For the measurement, either a high-speed camera or a CCD camera is 
used to take pictures of the given droplet at fixed intervals. A corre-
sponding software determines the coordinates of the drop profile by 
image analysis and sub-pixel edge detection, using cr and cz. The 
interfacial tension is then calculated from this information by fitting the 
profile to the Young-Laplace Equation, which relates the Laplace pres-
sure across an interface to the curvature of the interface and the inter-
facial tension [40].

The volume and area can be set as parameters to control the oscil-
latory deformations. In the case of area-based oscillations, the equip-
ment requires a feedback loop, where the area is constantly calculated 
from the drop image to make adjustments from the starting point i.e. the 
set area. In volume-based oscillations, regulation is not performed via 
image analysis but through communication between the motor and 
software, resulting in the controlled rotation of the motor shaft over 
time. For this reason, a volumetric calibration for the syringe, where the 
volume expelled by a syringe during one revolution of the motor is 
measured, is mandatory before starting the experiment. In other words, 
one has to measure the exact volume being pumped in/out by the motor 
before selecting a volume-based regulation for the experiment.
The downside of volume-based oscillations is that, as shown in the 
previous equation, the dilatational modulus is calculated based on area 
changes, and volume-based changes can result in less precise sinusoidal 
oscillations of the area. In particular, a drift from the set amplitude is 
possible between oscillation cycles i.e. the set value of the amplitude is 
not reached. On the other hand, a volume-based oscillation usually leads 
to a less noisy data set and allows for very small periods (as small as 1 % 
deformation amplitude), while small periods cannot be reached with an 
area-based oscillation. An area-controlled oscillation, however, mini-
mises deviations from the setpoint value. In short, each regulation 
method comes with advantages and disadvantages, and one should 
carefully choose a regulation type based on the required experimental 
setup. The volume-based or area-based regulation can also be used for 
simple surface tension measurements without oscillation to keep the 
area/ volume constant over time, e.g. during film formation.
2.3. Linear and nonlinear dilatational deformations
Three types of dilatational rheology tests are commonly performed: 
time, frequency, and amplitude sweeps. A time sweep allows one to 
follow the evolution of interfacial stiffness over time by plotting Eʹd and 
Eʹʹd as a function of time or surface pressure [45,46,19], where the latter 
is defined as Π0 = γ0  γ(t), and γ0 is the surface tension of the clean 
interface. This sweep is measured by subjecting the droplet or bubble to 
small oscillations with a fixed frequency and amplitude (Fig. 2, type 2).
It is worth noting that some literature refers to the monitoring of the 
dynamic surface tension over time also as “time sweep” tests, where no 
oscillations are performed, and thus this should be referred to as 
“interfacial tension” or “surface tension” measurements instead (Fig. 2, 
Time sweep type 1).
The earlier-mentioned interfacial/surface tension analysis can be 
combined with rheological tests, such as a frequency sweep, amplitude 
sweep, or step-dilation. In the section below, we will focus on the 
oscillatory rheology via frequency and amplitude sweeps. For more in-
formation about step dilatation, we refer the reader to [47]. First, the 
interface would be allowed to age, with typical times ranging from 1 to 
16 h, followed by a desired rheological test ([48,49,50,51,52]). Fre-
quency sweeps are used to assess the dependence of the dilatational 
moduli of the interface on frequency ω. Generally, the frequency of the 
deformation is increased, while keeping the amplitude constant. The 
dependence of the complex dilatational modulus on frequency can 
reveal information on the nature of interfacial viscoelasticity. In the 
absence of in-plane interactions, surfactants follow the Lucassen-Van 
den Tempel model [53], where the complex modulus is dominated by 
bulk-interface exchange. This model introduces a characteristic fre-
quency, ωc, which marks the transition between diffusion-controlled 
dynamics at lower frequencies and other interfacial processes domi-
nating at higher frequencies. Far below the characteristic frequency, the 
Lucassen-Van den Tempel model scales as Eʹd∼ ω0.5. For solid-like in-
terfaces, the frequency dependence of the Eʹd often scales in a power-law 
manner Eʹd∼ ωn, with n≪0.5. A low exponent n (~0.1–0.2) indicates the 
interface has the characteristics of a disordered solid (i.e., a 2D gel or 
jammed system), and is typically a sign that the interfacial structure 
displays a broad spectrum of relaxation times [54,55]. In such in-
terfaces, relaxation is not typically governed by bulk-interface exchange, 
and other processes play a more dominant role in the interfacial elas-
ticity, such as momentum transfer between interface and bulk or in- 
plane interactions [47]. For experiments using drop tensiometry, the 
maximum frequency that can be accurately applied is 0.1 Hz, whereas 
the lower limit employed is usually around 0.001 Hz, as lower fre-
quencies would increase the measuring time, thus introducing the ef-
fects of further ageing of the interface or adsorption of additional 
surface-active molecules. Faster frequencies up to 150 Hz can be 
reached using bubble pressure analysis in combination with a piezo- 
controlled motor [56].

Amplitude sweeps, where ω is kept constant, and the amplitude of 
oscillation is gradually increased from small to large deformations, are 
commonly used to find the limit of linearity, thus defining the linear 
(LVE) and nonlinear viscoelastic (NLVE) regimes [57,58,45,18,50]. This 
method is mostly used to ensure that other tests, such as time and fre-
quency sweeps, are performed within the linear regime. The interfacial 
viscoelasticity as a function of amplitude is determined by oscillating the 
droplet or air bubble with a fixed frequency (0.01 to 0.1 Hz) and varying 
amplitudes from usually 1–30 %. In amplitude sweeps, the applied fre-
quency and deformation regime are relatively narrow to avoid the for-
mation of surface waves [16]. In the LVE regime, it is assumed that the 
interfacial stress response amplitude is proportional to the applied 
deformation amplitude. The moduli are calculated from the intensity 
and phase of the first (or fundamental) harmonic in the frequency 
spectrum [16]. In this regard, the first harmonic is the input frequency of 
oscillation defined by the experimental settings.
Amplitude sweeps also provide a plethora of information when the 
deformations are performed in the NLVE regime, which goes beyond the 
quantification of Eʹd and Eʹʹd, which, as will be indicated below, are not 
accurate anymore. This corresponds to large amplitude oscillatory 
dilatation (LAOD). Interfaces show a nonlinear dynamic response 
beyond a critical amplitude as a consequence of the changes in the 
interfacial microstructure induced by the area changes. This nonlinear 
behaviour conveys additional information about the interfacial visco-
elasticity and its stability under real process conditions.
In the NLVE regime, the surface stress response can be decomposed 
into a superposition of sinusoidal waves, starting with the applied fre-
quency and including positive integer multiples of this frequency, 
known as higher harmonics. Using the Fourier Transformation, the 
contribution of such higher harmonics to the stress signal can be accu-
rately quantified. Each of these higher harmonics has its own amplitude 
and phase angle [57]. Analysing highly nonlinear behaviour in dilata-
tional experiments can pose challenges, as the applied deformation may 
become non-affine, as demonstrated for very stiff (hyper)elastic inter-
facial films [59]. The result is a non-uniform, thus non-Laplacian shape 
of the droplet, thereby hindering the accurate analysis of surface stress 
upon oscillatory deformations [60]. Examples of a Laplacian and non- 
Laplacian droplet shape are shown in Fig. 3. In such cases, capillary 
meniscus dynamometry offers better results. This method uses both the 
shape of the meniscus and the internal capillary pressure to calculate the 
components of the surface stress in the meridian or parallel directions 
[61].
When looking at the literature, for time sweeps and frequency 
sweeps, it is often tacitly assumed that the data was obtained within the 
LVE regime, and, therefore, that the surface tension signal follows a 
simple sine wave, which can be Fourier transformed to obtain Eʹd and E˝d 
from the amplitude and phase of the first harmonic [62]. If these tests 
are performed outside of the LVE regime, then Eʹd and E˝d become inac-
curate, due to the presence of higher harmonics in the signal [16]. Un-
fortunately, the extent of the LVE is often not checked in studies that 
perform dilatational rheology, and frequency or time sweeps are 
sometimes performed at amplitudes as large as 20 %. These measure-
ments might well be in the nonlinear regime, therefore making the ob-
tained Eʹd and Eʹʹd values questionable [16]. A combination of tests within 
a study is, therefore, the most recommended approach. (Rheological) 
time sweeps are used to evaluate a proper plateau for the ageing of the 
interface, ensuring the interface does not evolve to a large extent during 
a frequency or amplitude sweep, which would render the results inac-
curate. Amplitude sweeps should then be used to evaluate the extent of 
the LVE regime and analyse the behaviour of the interface under large 
deformations. Finally, frequency sweeps can be used to study the time- 
dependent rheological behaviour of the interface at an amplitude in the 
LVE regime.
Since the dynamic moduli lose their physical meaning beyond the 
LVE, other approaches are necessary to evaluate the nonlinear defor-
mation behaviour within the LAOD regime, which will be discussed in 
the next sections.

stress signal is no longer a simple sinusoidal response, but consists of 
contributions at the applied testing frequency and terms at multiples of 
this frequency [63,16] (Fig. 4). Some (modern) drop tensiometer

A similar issue was reported for interfacial large amplitude oscilla-
tory shear (LAOS) experiments [20,58]. Like in dilatational rheology, in 
LAOS a sinusoidal deformation is applied to an interface at various 
strains and single frequencies. For LAOS, different methods have been 
developed to quantify the nonlinear oscillatory stress response in the 
nonlinear regime, such as the use of more advanced FT-algorithms, 
which e.g. allow averaging the magnitude spectra so that the peaks 
are all positive and independent of the phase of the higher harmonics 
[64]. Another approach is the introduction of a nonlinear quantitative 
coefficient, Q, within the FT analysis to evaluate the intensity and 
variation of the higher harmonics compared to the first harmonic [60].
Unfortunately, these approaches cannot simply be adopted for dila-
tational rheology, as stress-controlled rheometers have a much lower 
torque limit (thus higher sensitivities) than image-based tensiometers, 
making the FT analysis more robust. Additionally, dilatational rheology 
follows slightly different mathematical and physical principles. One 
main difference between surface shear and dilatational rheology is the 
area conservation of the interface. While in surface shear rheology, the 
total area is conserved, this is not the case in dilatational rheology (area 
increases/decreases continuously during oscillation and with it, the 
density of the adsorbed emulsifier). Mathematically speaking, only odd 
harmonics are included in shear rheology because the stress response 
can be assumed to be of odd symmetry with respect to the directionality 
of shear strain or shear rate [20]. In contrast, both, even and odd har-
monics appear in LAOD due to changes in density upon expansion and 
contraction of the interface. In other words, there is no odd symmetry, 
and the surface tension signal will look different in compression and 
expansion. Other methods are needed to quantify the nonlinear material 
response under LAOD deformation, which will be further discussed in 
Section 3.
2.5. A more fundamental look at interfacial dynamics: The surface 
momentum balance
The origin of the nonlinearities we discussed in the preceding sec-
tions may not be immediately obvious. We discuss this here in more 
detail by taking a closer look at the dynamics of an interface when it is 
deformed.
In principle, a drop tensiometer measures surface tension, and the 
dilatational modulus we determine by applying oscillatory area changes 
using Eq. (6), in which the numerator for most simple surfactant- 
stabilised interfaces contains only contributions from surface tension. 
But many surface-active components from viscoelastic solid-like 
(arrested) structures, after adsorption (i.e., 2D networks, soft solid 
glasses, or solid crystalline phases), and upon expansion/compression of 
these structures, deviatoric stresses are induced. From Eq. (6), it is not 
immediately clear how these stresses are accounted for in the mea-
surement. These deviatoric stresses may also result in the emergence of 
surface shear stresses in the measured surface stress, particularly for 
very stiff interfaces with a high surface shear modulus. The dilatational 
deformation in a drop tensiometer is, in fact, not a completely all-sided 
deformation. In very stiff and highly elastic interfaces, the deformations 
in the meridional and azimuthal directions of the drop may deviate 
significantly, resulting in a substantial shear deformation. To see how 
these effects emerge in the measured surface stress signal we have to 
take a closer look at the Young-Laplace equation.

The deviatoric stresses in the effective surface tension are not the 
only source of nonlinearities in the response. The surface tension itself is 
dependent on the surface load of the adsorbed component through an 
equation of state, which is in general a nonlinear equation, even for the 
simplest types of interfaces, i.e., interfaces described by the 2D ideal gas 
law. The surface tension will increase upon expansion and decrease 
upon compression. As a result of the nonlinearity of the equation of 
state, when deformations are sufficiently large, these effects are not 
symmetric with respect to the nondeformed state, leading to the emer-
gence of the even harmonics, which we mentioned in the previous 
section.
3. Qualitative and quantitative analysis of LAOD
3.1. Lissajous plots
In view of our discussion in the previous section, other methods are 
needed for the analysis of the nonlinear material response in LAOD 
deformations. A popular qualitative analysis of LAOD is by plotting the 
measured cyclic surface stress/pressure directly against the time- 
dependent area signal in Lissajous(-Bowditch) plots ([20,50,67,46]). 
In these plots, the surface stress changes in a clockwise direction, and at 
sufficiently small amplitudes, the plots will be point-symmetric with 
respect to the origin (Fig. 5). Here, the upper part of the plot is the 
expansion part of the deformation cycle, where the area changes from its 
minimum to its maximum value. The opposite is true for the bottom part 
of the plot, which represents the compression part of the deformation 
cycle. The shape of the Lissajous plots showcases the rheological 
response. In a purely elastic response, the stress is in phase with the 
amplitude, resulting in a straight line in the plots. In a purely viscous 
response, the stress response is 90◦ out of phase with the applied 
amplitude, creating a circle in the plots. Viscoelastic behaviour is shown 
as a combination of both elastic and viscous signals, in the form of an 
ellipsoidal shape. In the NLVE regime, Lissajous plots will show a 
distortion from the original symmetric shape where the plots can show 
different responses in expansion or compression as a result of cyclic 
interfacial strain stiffening and softening, as shown in the example, 
where Lissajous plots become more asymmetric at higher deformations 
(Fig. 5).
The strain stiffening and softening visually shown in Lissajous plots 
was, for instance, quantified in Van Kempen et al. [46], where the 
intracycle strain stiffening ratio in compression Scom and expansion Sexp 
was analysed, in the same way as [20] defined the intracycle strain 
stiffening ratio S for bulk rheology. Such a method is a geometrical 
interpretation calculated from the shape of Lissajous curves, and the 
higher harmonic components obtained via Fourier transformation of the 
oscillatory signal are not used. A more accurate method to quantify the 
nonlinear contributions in LAOD, a general stress decomposition (GSD) 
method was developed by de Groot et al., [25], and is described in the 
next section.

inflexion points in the surface pressure isotherm. The isotropy of inter-

surfactants are known to go through phase transitions under compres-

extremely large deformations for specific surfactants [72,70]. Never-

4. Application of LAOD to investigate the nonlinear deformation 
behaviour of various emulsifiers – Examples
In this section, we will show the potential of LAOD in elucidating the 
mechanical properties of liquid-liquid or air-liquid interfaces. LAOD can 
give insights into the type of interfacial microstructures formed and the 
type of in-plane interactions between adsorbed interfacial stabilisers. 
We will discuss several categories of stabilisers, such as low molecular 
weight surfactants, proteins and particles.
LAOD is especially powerful in combination with microstructure 
imaging, as the latter can underpin the microscopic structural features in 
interfacial films suggested by the LAOD results. Examples are Langmuir- 
Blodgett or – Schaefer deposition of interfacial layers onto substrates, 
followed by atomic force microscopy (AFM) or electron microscopy 
([75,76]; Q. [77,14]). Less invasive and more in-situ methods include 
Brewster angle microscopy (BAM) in combination with a Langmuir 
through or multiphoton microscopy [78,79,80]. Examples where LAOD 
and imaging have been used for different emulsifiers such as surfactants, 
proteins and particles are shown in Table 1. A category of mixtures is 
also shown, as LAOD has been proven useful in underpinning the role of 
different emulsifiers in mixed systems.
4.1. Surfactants
Low molecular weight surfactants are generally amphiphilic mole-
cules with a well-defined hydrophilic headgroup and hydrophobic tail. 
In literature, most surfactants are not expected to self-assemble into 
interfacial 2D microstructures and are, therefore, assumed to form weak 
and highly viscous interfacial films which are unstable under large de-
formations. At low deformation rates, their dilatational properties are 
largely governed by dynamic exchange of the surfactant between 
interface and the bulk solution. However, under fast deformations, the 
exchange of surfactant between interface and bulk cannot keep up with 
the deformation, as described by the Lucassen-van den Tempel theory 
[53]. In such cases, the interfacial response appears elastic not because 
of structural rigidity, but due to the inability of the adsorbed surfactant 
molecules to rapidly move to the bulk phase. Nevertheless, recent 
studies have shown that some surfactants (e.g. phospholipids, saponins) 
may form viscoelastic networks at the interface with complex nonlinear 
behaviour [102,103,104,105,106].
So far, only a few studies have used GSD to analyse the interfacial 
behaviour of surfactant-stabilised interfacial films. For example, De 
Groot et al. reported nonlinear in-plane behaviour for lecithin. Here, the 
GSD showed that in-plane network interactions are nearly absent when 
deforming lecithin-stabilised air-water interfaces, and the surface den-
sity changes dominated the overall stress signal [25]. Risse et al. 
expanded the investigation of the nonlinear behaviour of lecithin, which 
in fact are phospholipid mixtures, and showed that unsaturated phos-
pholipids lack in-plane interactions at the oil-water interface. In 
contrast, saturated phospholipids formed stiff interfacial films with 
dominating in-plane network interactions [49]. The difference between 
unsaturated and saturated phospholipids comes from the wider fatty 
acid tails of the unsaturated phospholipids that provide steric hindrance 
at the interface, resulting in less tight packing of the head groups at the 
interface. Similar behaviour was also shown for the surfactants Tween, 
Span and Brij, which have different head- and tail-group sizes [83].
Escin is a triterpenoid bidesmosidic (2 sugar residues) saponin which 
forms stiff air-water interfaces with dilatational (and shear) viscoelastic 
moduli of 1 to 2 orders of magnitude larger than those of proteins at the 
air-water [72,107,104,95]. The stiff interfacial films resulted in non- 
Laplacian droplet shapes at larger deformations, which were reflected 
in highly nonlinear Lissajous plots. The Lissajous plots of escin showed a

4.2. Proteins
Proteins are biopolymers, of which, many are surface-active. Surface 
active proteins can adsorb at the interface, followed by structural rear-
rangements and interactions, finally, forming viscoelastic films 
[109,10,110]. The type of interface formed depends largely on the type 
of interactions and interfacial arrangements of adsorbed proteins, which 
can be effectively analysed using LAOD [89,85,18,84]. An example was 
shown for rapeseed proteins, where air-water interfacial films of rape-
seed proteins, specifically napin and cruciferin, were studied [84]. 
Napin is a smaller more hydrophilic protein, which forms thinner and 
weaker interfacial structures. Cruciferin proteins, which are about 20 
times larger than napin and more hydrophobic, can form stiffer inter-
facial layers. This also resulted in a higher stability of cruciferin- 
stabilised foams than napin-stabilised ones. These insights were ob-
tained using a combination of LAOD rheology and microstructural im-
aging of the interface. Examples of atomic force microscopy images of 
Langmuir-Blodgett films of cruciferin and napin are shown in Fig. 10. 
Here, one can observe a heterogeneous structure for cruciferin, sug-
gesting a gel-like structure, while napin showed a thinner and more 
homogenous structure. The combination of interfacial rheology and 
imaging has been useful in explaining interfacial microstructural dif-
ferences between proteins, such as dairy proteins, plant proteins, and 
peptides [18,111,112,113,80].
The GSD has been applied for several protein-stabilised interfaces (e. 
g., rubisco and casein proteins). Ma et al. extracted rubisco proteins from 
spinach using two different methodologies [85]. Proteins that were 
mildly extracted retained their native structure, and these proteins were

4.3. Mixtures
LAOD has been proven useful in understanding the role of individual 
components in multicomponent systems, such as protein-protein and 
protein-surfactant mixtures. When both stabilisers are initially present 
in the bulk phase, this may lead to competitive adsorption (one stabiliser 
dominates the interface), co-adsorption (both adsorb forming homoge-
nous or heterogenous blends at the interface), and/or displacement (one 
stabiliser replaces the other at the interface over time) [114]. For 
instance, caseinate-tannic acid mixtures formed stiffer interfaces than 
caseinate and tannic acid alone [93]. The molecules interacted and 
formed complexes that co-adsorbed, where the tannic acid probably 
further connected the proteins after adsorption, yielding stiffer in-
terfaces. Co-adsorption without interactions is also possible, as shown 
for whey protein and pea protein mixtures, where both proteins adsor-
bed onto the interface, and hindered each other’s network formation 
[75]. Sequential adsorption can be studied by first forming an interface 
in a drop tensiometer with the first stabiliser, followed by the exchange 
of the water or oil phase, and introducing a second stabiliser. Performing 
LAOD before and after the exchange gives insights into the potential 
displacement of the first stabiliser by the second one [115].
Interfaces stabilised by mixtures of proteins and smaller surfactants 
are especially suitable for analysis using LAOD. The first example is a 
whey protein-escin mixture, where, at low escin concentrations, the 
escin can increase the stiffness of the mixture ([107]; J. [95]). At high 
escin concentrations, escin will dominate the interfacial behaviour of 
the mixtures. The opposite is also possible, as shown for rapeseed 
protein-oleosome mixtures [94]. Oleosomes are oil droplets stabilised 
with phospholipids and membrane proteins, and upon adsorption at the 
interface, the droplets can rupture, spreading the oil, phospholipids and 
membrane proteins. In a protein-oleosome mixture, the oleosome 
rupture at the interface can significantly reduce the formation of stiff 
protein-stabilised interfaces [94]. If protein concentrations are high 
enough, the proteins can outcompete the adsorption of oleosomes at the 
air-water interface, thus preserving the protein-stabilised interface. In 
both the whey protein-escin and rapeseed protein-oleosome mixtures, 
the combination of imaging and rheology was essential to obtain the 
earlier-mentioned insights.
The GSD has also revealed specific insights on mixed systems. For 
example, in oleosome membrane proteins and phospholipid mixtures, 
the proteins formed stiff viscoelastic interfaces, and the phospholipids 
formed weak stretchable interfaces (J. [96]). Upon mixing, nearly 
identical Lissajous plots were observed for the mixtures at several ratios, 
suggesting that oleosins dominated the interface. But, after performing 
the GSD, a clear contribution of the phospholipids was observed, sug-
gesting the presence of both components at the interface. A final 
example is an oleosome membrane-curcumin mixture-stabilised oil- 
water interface [97], where an increase of surface elastic modulus was 
observed at increasing deformation amplitude from 5 to 30 %, followed 
by a modulus decrease when increasing to 50 % deformation. This in-
crease in modulus is unexpected, as higher deformation typically dis-
rupts the interfacial microstructure, resulting in lower modulus values. 
Here, the GSD showed a continued increase of network interactions over 
the whole deformation range, while the surface density changes showed 
a similar increase followed by a decrease. These findings suggest that the 
interfacial network strength is increasing, likely due to newly adsorbed 
material being constantly compressed into a denser layer. At the same 
time, the density contribution increased, but decreased again at de-
formations >30 %. Ultimately, at >30 % deformations, the interfacial 
structure was broken down, resulting in an overall decrease in moduli. 
In both cases, the GSD was essential in gaining insights into the interface 
stabilising role of individual components in a multicomponent system.
4.4. Particles
Particles have been widely studied as interface stabilisers, often 
referred to as Pickering stabilisers, and have been studied using LAOD. 
Here, a main caution is the actual presence of particles at the interface in 
a diffusion-based system, as shown for protein-silica bead systems, 
where the beads sediment and change the droplet’s shape, leading to an 
apparent surface tension decrease [116]. Sometimes, particles cannot 
spontaneously adsorb at an interface and attach only by convection, for 
instance, by using homogenisation. Several studies have demonstrated 
the absence of particles at the interface in tensiometry, as observed for 
soy protein fibrils [99]. These large fibrils were not surface-active, and 
the non-aggregated proteins in the solution dominated the interfacial

If the particles are actually adsorbing at the interface, LAOD can give 
insights into the type of layers formed. For example, for cellulose 
nanocrystals stabilised interfaces, salt screened the particle’s charges

were obtained using neutron reflectometry. The GSD was used to study a 
gliadin nanoparticle system [23]. LAOD deformations were performed 
at different ageing times of the gliadin nanoparticle-stabilised interface. 
In combination with imaging, the authors showed how the nanoparticles 
transformed over time at the interface by slowly deforming and merging 
into a viscoelastic solid polymeric film.
5. Summary and future outlook
Large amplitude oscillatory dilatational (LAOD) rheology has 
emerged as a powerful technique for characterising the mechanical 
behaviour of interfaces under nonlinear deformation conditions. LAOD 
enables us to probe the complex viscoelastic properties of interfacial 
films stabilised by a broad range of surface-active molecules, including 
low molecular weight surfactants, proteins, particles, and their mix-
tures. These insights are crucial for understanding and predicting the 
stability of emulsions, foams, and other multiphase systems. For de-
cades, the analysis of LAOD data was limited to first-harmonic-based 
moduli and Lissajous plots. While these methods provided valuable 
qualitative insights, they struggled to accurately quantify nonlinear 
behaviour, especially when higher harmonic contributions became sig-
nificant. As such, first harmonic-based moduli in the nonlinear visco-
elastic (NLVE) regime often led to incomplete or misleading 
interpretations of interfacial mechanics. The recent development of the 
general stress decomposition (GSD) has been a significant breakthrough. 
This new method enables a quantitative separation of rheological 
(network) contributions and surface density-driven effects in the stress 
response. By isolating the true rheological signal, GSD opens the door to 
a much more detailed and mechanistic understanding of interfacial 
behaviour. It allows rheological analyses that were previously not 
possible, thereby transforming the field of LAOD.
As the field advances, several promising research directions emerge: 
• Revisiting classic systems: Many interfacial films previously 
characterised with traditional methods should be re-evaluated using 
LAOD in combination with GSD. This could reveal overlooked non-
linearities or misinterpreted behaviours.
• Expanding GSD applications: More interfacial systems, especially 
complex and multicomponent interfaces, should be studied using 
GSD to create a broader understanding of interfacial mechanics 
across different materials.
• New rheological models: Since GSD effectively decouples surface 
density changes from intrinsic rheology, the pure rheological signal 
obtained can serve as a foundation for new constitutive models of 
interfacial rheology. An example would be models to describe 
anisotropic interfacial behaviour.
• Dynamic surface pressure isotherms: The density-driven contri-
bution identified using GSD directly links to the dynamic surface 
pressure behaviour. Systematically exploring this relationship could 
provide a better understanding of the origin of the formation of 
specific microstructures, such as those observed in protein-stabilised 
interfaces.
Together, these advances show that LAOD, especially in combination 
with GSD, gives a comprehensive framework for measuring and un-
derstanding nonlinear interfacial mechanics.
